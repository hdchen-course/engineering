The Landscape — What These SWE Roles Actually Build

Software, not firmware. The job is to make a warehouse of accelerators behave like one programmable computer.

The one-sentence analogy: a single accelerator is like one very fast chef. A modern model is a banquet no single chef can cook — the ingredients (weights) don't even fit on one counter (one chip's memory). So you hire thousands of chefs, and you need a kitchen operating system: someone to split the recipe, route ingredients between stations, keep everyone in sync, replace a chef who faints mid-service, and tell you which station is the slow one. That kitchen OS is the software these engineers write. The chefs (silicon) and their reflexes (firmware) are someone else's job.

THE LINE THIS CHAPTER DRAWS

Firmware/hardware = the chip's microcode, the NVLink/ICI PHY, the memory controller, the RTL. ML-systems software (this chapter) = everything from the CUDA/XLA kernel up: collective libraries, training frameworks, inference servers, schedulers, IaC control planes, telemetry pipelines, and profilers. If it ships as a container, a binary, a Terraform module, or a PromQL query — it's this chapter. If it ships as a bitstream or a driver you flash, it's not.

Tiny worked example: why you can't just "buy a bigger chip"

A 70-billion-parameter model in 16-bit precision needs 70e9 × 2 bytes = 140 GB just for weights. Training needs far more: optimizer state for Adam is ~12 bytes/param (fp32 momentum + variance + primary copy) → ~840 GB, plus gradients and activations. A top GPU has ~80–192 GB of memory. So even inference can need multiple chips — 140 GB of weights already overflows an 80 GB GPU, and the KV cache, activations, and batch headroom push past even a 192 GB one — and training needs dozens-to-thousands, wired so they can act as one. Every topic in this chapter falls out of that single fact.

Collective Communication — How N Chips Agree on One Number

All-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter. The tax on every parallelism strategy, and the thing that decides whether 1,000 chips act like 1,000 or like 10.

Analogy: four students each grade a quarter of the same exam pile and must end up knowing the total class average. Naive plan: everyone shouts their sum to everyone (chaos, and the loudest hallway — the network — is the bottleneck). Smarter plan: pass partial sums around a circle so each hop adds a little, and by the time the parcel has gone around, everyone holds the full total. That circle is ring all-reduce, and it is how gradients get summed across GPUs after every training step.

The four collectives you must name

All-Reduce

Every device ends with the element-wise sum (or mean/max) of all devices' arrays. Used to average gradients in data parallelism.

Key: reduce-scatter + all-gather
All-Gather

Each device holds a shard; afterward every device holds the full concatenation. Used to reassemble sharded weights (FSDP) before a layer.

Key: no math, just collect
Reduce-Scatter

Sum across devices, but each keeps only its slice of the result. Used to shard gradients (ZeRO).

Key: reduce, keep 1/N
All-to-All

Every device sends a distinct piece to every other. Used to route tokens to experts in MoE.

Key: the MoE / expert-parallel primitive
THE KEY IDENTITY

All-Reduce = Reduce-Scatter then All-Gather. That is why the animation below has two phases. It's also why the ring all-reduce moves 2×(N−1)/N × data bytes per device — roughly twice the data size, independent of N. That N-independence is the whole reason it scales.

Watch it happen: ring all-reduce, step by step

Four devices (D0–D3), each starts with an array split into 4 chunks. Phase 1 (reduce-scatter): 3 steps of "send a chunk to your right neighbor, who adds it in" — after which each device owns one fully-summed chunk. Phase 2 (all-gather): 3 more steps circulate those finished chunks so everyone ends with all four sums. Press Step.

Senior depth: overlap communication with compute

A collective that runs after compute finishes is dead time — the accelerator's ALUs idle while the network works. The single biggest lever in distributed training is overlap: start all-reducing the gradients of layer L while you're still computing the backward pass of layer L−1. Frameworks do this by bucketing gradients (group small tensors into ~25–50 MB buckets so each collective is big enough to hit peak bandwidth) and launching the collective on a separate CUDA/stream as soon as a bucket is ready.

# Overlap: fire the collective as soon as a gradient bucket fills,
# so comms of bucket k hides behind compute of bucket k+1.
for layer in reversed(model.layers):      # backward pass, last layer first
    grads = layer.backward()
    bucket.add(grads)
    if bucket.full(25 * MB):          # big enough to saturate the link
        allreduce_async(bucket, stream=comm_stream)   # non-blocking
        bucket = Bucket()
if not bucket.empty():                 # flush the trailing partial bucket
    allreduce_async(bucket, stream=comm_stream)
sync(comm_stream)                        # only wait at the very end
WHERE IT GOES WRONG

Buckets too small → latency-bound, you never reach peak bandwidth, and launch overhead dominates. Buckets too large → less overlap (you wait for a big bucket to fill before comms can start) and a bigger tail. Collective crosses a slow link (e.g. a tensor-parallel all-reduce that spills off NVLink onto InfiniBand) → step time spikes. This is why the topology section exists.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Expect: "Your training doesn't speed up when you add nodes — why?" Crisp answer: "First suspect is collectives. I'd check (1) are all-reduces overlapped with backward or serialized after it; (2) is the collective staying inside the fast interconnect domain or crossing DCN/IB mid-layer; (3) bucket sizing. I'd confirm with per-rank in-collective time and link counters — and remember a synchronous all-reduce gates every rank on the slowest, so the symptom looks identical whether the cause is a straggler, a slow link, or load imbalance."

Distributed-Training Parallelism & Sharding

Data, tensor, pipeline, and expert parallelism; FSDP/ZeRO. Four ways to cut one model across many chips — usually combined.

Analogy: printing a giant newspaper. Data parallelism = many identical printing presses, each printing different copies, then you reconcile the run counts (all-reduce). Tensor parallelism = one press so big you split a single page across machines that must hand off within every page. Pipeline parallelism = an assembly line where each station prints certain pages and passes the paper along. Expert parallelism = specialist stations, and each article is routed only to the station that handles its topic (MoE). Real training stacks combine all four.

Worked memory math: why one chip can't hold the model

Take a 70B model, mixed-precision Adam training on 80 GB GPUs:

ItemFormulaSize
Weights (bf16)70e9 × 2140 GB
Gradients (bf16)70e9 × 2140 GB
Optimizer state (fp32 primary + m + v)70e9 × 12840 GB
Subtotal (states)~1,120 GB
Activations (batch/seq-dependent)varies10s–100s GB

~1.1 TB of state on 80 GB chips → you need at least 14 chips just to hold it, before any batch. Replicating (plain data parallelism) is impossible — the model doesn't fit once. This is what forces sharding.

FSDP / ZeRO — shard the state, not just the batch

Adam's optimizer state (12 B/param) uses by far the most memory. ZeRO-1 shards optimizer state; ZeRO-2 adds gradients; ZeRO-3 / FSDP adds parameters. Under FSDP each rank stores only 1/N of everything; just before a layer runs it all-gathers that layer's full weights, uses them, then frees them — trading extra communication for a huge memory win. That per-layer all-gather is exactly the collective from the previous tab, and it must overlap with compute or it becomes the bottleneck.

How a transformer is split across the four axes

Sizing rules (say these numbers out loud)

  • Tensor parallelism: sized to the minimum degree that fits weights + working memory, then kept inside one high-bandwidth domain (8 GPUs on NVLink, or one ICI slice). It costs ~2 all-reduces per layer per forward pass (4 counting the backward pass) — cross a slow link and you're done.
  • Pipeline parallelism: the tool when even TP can't fit the model in one NVLink domain. The cost is the bubble — stages idle during fill/drain. Bubble fraction ≈ (P−1)/(m+P−1) for P stages and m micro-batches; more micro-batches shrink it.
  • Data parallelism / FSDP: the outermost axis, for throughput; scale replicas here once the model fits.
  • Expert parallelism: for MoE, spreads experts across devices; the routing all-to-all and load imbalance across experts are the hard parts.
ELASTIC RESHARDING PITFALL

Resuming a job at a different world size changes the global batch size, which changes convergence — unless you hold global batch constant via gradient accumulation. "Just reshard to whatever nodes are free" is a correctness hazard, not a free win.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Classic prompt: "This model won't fit — how do you split it?" Strong answer walks the memory math first (weights + grads + optimizer), then: "FSDP/ZeRO-3 to shard state; TP inside the NVLink domain for the layers that need it; PP across domains if still too big; DP on the outside for throughput. I'd map the chattiest axis (TP) to the fastest fabric and the least-chatty (DP) to the slowest, and overlap every collective with compute." Naming the 4-D composition and the fabric mapping is the staff signal.

Inference Runtime & Serving

Continuous batching, KV cache & paged attention, latency-vs-throughput scheduling, and quantization. Where throughput becomes dollars.

Analogy: a restaurant kitchen. Static batching = you wait until 8 tables have ordered, cook all 8 dishes together, and serve them only when the slowest dish is done — fast eaters wait, the grill idles. Continuous batching = the moment any dish leaves the pass, you pull the next order onto the grill. The grill (the accelerator) never idles, and diners aren't held hostage by the table ordering the 40-course tasting menu.

Prefill vs decode — two different machines

PhaseWorkBound byMetric it drives
PrefillProcess the whole prompt at once, build the KV cacheCompute-bound (big GEMMs over all prompt tokens)TTFT (time to first token)
DecodeGenerate one token per step, append to KVMemory-bandwidth-bound (re-stream every weight to make one token)TPOT (time per output token)
THE SPINE OF SERVING

Decode is memory-bound because generating one token reads the entire weight set from HBM to do a tiny matrix–vector product — arithmetic intensity ~1–2, far below the ridge point (the FLOP/byte where a chip flips from memory-bound to compute-bound; see the Profiling tab). So decode time per token ≈ model_bytes_read / HBM_bandwidth, nearly independent of FLOPs. That's why bigger batches are almost free on compute during decode (you amortize the weight read across many sequences) — until you run out of KV-cache memory. Batching is the throughput/$ lever; HBM is the ceiling.

KV cache & paged attention

Attention needs the Key/Value vectors of every previous token, cached in HBM so each new token isn't recomputed from scratch. Size grows linearly with context: 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × seq_len × batch × dtype. For a 70B-class model with grouped-query attention that's roughly ~300 KB per token — so a 128K-context request can need ~40 GB of KV, half a big GPU, for a single request.

FIXPaged attention — KV cache as OS-style virtual memoryHARD▶

Naive engines pre-allocate a contiguous KV buffer per slot sized to the max sequence length. With a 150-token average output you reserve for 128K and use ~0.1% — catastrophic internal fragmentation that caps concurrency to a handful of slots. Paged attention allocates KV in fixed-size blocks (e.g. 16 tokens) via a block table, exactly like virtual-memory paging: KV grows on demand, so you hold only what you use. Two bonuses: (1) far higher concurrency → bigger batches → better utilization; (2) prefix sharing — identical system prompts / few-shot / conversation history share physical blocks copy-on-write, saving both HBM and re-prefill compute.

Scheduling for latency AND throughput

  • Chunked prefill: a 32K prompt is a compute-bound burst; drop it whole into a decode step and every decoder's TPOT spikes (head-of-line blocking). Split the prefill into 512–2K-token chunks and interleave with decode steps so decoders keep getting ~40 ms tokens.
  • Prefill/decode disaggregation: run prefill on a compute-optimized pool and decode on a bandwidth-optimized pool, handing off the KV cache — but that hand-off is ~10 GB for a long prompt, so it only pays on a fast fabric.
  • Speculative decoding: a small draft model proposes k tokens; the big model verifies all k in one forward pass and accepts the longest correct prefix — provably distribution-preserving (token-identical only under greedy decoding), 2–3× fewer big-model steps when acceptance is high. Free because decode has spare compute while waiting on HBM.

Quantization: the precision/quality trade

FormatBitsUseTrade-off
FP16 / BF1616Default training & serving. BF16 has wider range, less mantissa.Baseline quality; 2 bytes/param.
FP8 (E4M3 / E5M2)8Serving weights + activations on modern HW; doubles peak FLOP/s too.~2× throughput on memory-bound decode; needs per-tensor scales, watch outliers.

WHY IT'S THE #1 COST LEVER

Decode is bandwidth-bound, so halving weight bytes (FP16→FP8) buys close to 2× throughput and frees HBM for more KV (more concurrency). Quantizing the KV cache directly multiplies how many sequences fit. Always name quantization when asked to cut $/token.

Cluster Scheduling & Topology-Aware Placement

Gang scheduling, SLURM vs Kubernetes, queueing, and mapping collectives onto NVLink / InfiniBand / RoCE / ICI.

Analogy: seating a rowing team. A synchronized 8-person crew is useless if you seat 7 and leave one on the dock — the boat can't move and you've wasted 7 seats. You must seat all 8 together (gang scheduling) and seat them in the same boat, not scattered across the lake (topology-aware placement), because they have to stroke in unison every second.

Gang scheduling — all-or-nothing

Synchronous training with collectives deadlocks if even one worker is missing: the other N−1 block forever at the first all-reduce, burning reserved accelerators. Gang scheduling places every worker of a job together or none at all. Vanilla kube-scheduler doesn't do this — it will happily place 7 of 8 pods and leave the job hung — which is why the ecosystem uses gang-aware schedulers (Volcano, Kueue) or SLURM.

HYBRID REALITY

Real "supercomputing software" roles run both: SLURM for the big training campaigns, Kubernetes for serving and services, with a control plane (often Terraform/OpenTofu-provisioned) stitching them over shared storage and a shared identity/quota layer. Knowing when each wins — and how to give users one submit surface over both — is the platform job.

Queueing: fairness without starvation

  • Fair-share + priority tiers: higher tier can preempt lower, but preemption is checkpoint-aware — snapshot the victim, then resume it, never restart from zero.
  • Backfill: small 1-GPU debug jobs fill the fragmentation gaps while a 512-GPU gang is still assembling — utilization without delaying the big job.
  • Quota borrowing with reclaim: idle org-B capacity is borrowable by org-A and reclaimable on demand — high utilization without starving the owner.
  • Legible queue: show position, ETA, and who is blocking you. Making the queue transparent is itself the anti-revolt feature.

Topology-aware placement — the bandwidth cliff

An 8-GPU tensor-parallel job placed on one node talks over NVLink/NVSwitch (hundreds of GB/s to TB/s). The same job scattered across 8 nodes does its all-reduce over InfiniBand — and if GPUDirect RDMA isn't set up, it even bounces through host memory. Since InfiniBand's tens of GB/s is roughly an order of magnitude below NVLink's hundreds of GB/s to TB/s, that bandwidth-bound all-reduce slows by about that much from placement alone. The scheduler must understand the fabric:

RAIL-OPTIMIZED & SHARP

Big GPU clusters wire NICs into rails so same-rank GPUs across nodes share a switch, keeping collectives on short paths. Some switches also do in-network reduction (NVIDIA SHARP), summing partial gradients inside the switch so data doesn't round-trip to every endpoint — a hardware assist the software must opt into. On TPUs, an optical circuit switch (OCS) physically reconfigures the torus per job to carve contiguous slices and route around failed chips.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Probe: "8-GPU job, but the fleet is fragmented (40 nodes with 1 free GPU each) — what do you do?" Crisp: "Never bin-pack a gang across singles — that turns an NVLink all-reduce into an IB one — roughly an order of magnitude slower for that bandwidth-bound collective. Reliability & Observability at Fleet Scale

Failure detection, node draining, straggler mitigation, silent data corruption, and the metrics pipeline that catches it all.

Analogy: a relay race where all runners must cross the line together to score, run daily for weeks. At thousands of runners, someone will pull a hamstring every day — so you need instant injury detection, a bench of substitutes, and a rule to restart from the last checkpoint rather than the start. And the scariest failure isn't a runner collapsing (loud) — it's a runner quietly running the wrong route while looking fine (silent corruption).

The scale-driven fact: failure is constant

If one accelerator has a mean time between failures of, say, 50,000 hours, a 10,000-chip job hits a failure roughly every 50000/10000 = 5 hours. A synchronous job means one dead rank stalls all 10,000. So fault tolerance isn't a nice-to-have; it's the difference between a run finishing and a run that never completes.

The reliability loop

Distributed checkpointing & fault tolerance

  • Sharded checkpointing: each rank writes its own shard in parallel to object storage — no funneling terabytes through one writer.
  • Async checkpointing: snapshot state to pinned host memory, then flush to storage in the background so the accelerators keep stepping — the copy-out stall, not the disk write, is what you hide.
  • Checkpoint interval: not hand-waved — the Young/Daly optimum is interval ≈ √(2 × checkpoint_cost × MTBF). Too frequent wastes compute; too rare wastes re-do work on failure.
  • Deterministic resume: must include dataloader position + RNG state, or you silently re-see or skip data.
  • Elastic training: continue at a smaller world size after a failure (hold global batch constant), rather than blocking for a replacement.

Straggler mitigation

Because a synchronous all-reduce gates everyone on the slowest rank, one thermally-throttled GPU, a NUMA-misplaced process, or a slow NIC drags the whole job. Detection is a per-rank step-time histogram: a single outlier that is slow in compute (while others are slow in collective, i.e. blocked at the barrier) is a straggler host; uniformly elevated collective time points at a slow link; structured spread across many ranks points at load imbalance. Fixes: reschedule off the bad host, rebalance sharding, or in some frameworks let the collective proceed without the laggard.

SDCSilent data corruption — the failure that doesn't alert youHARD▶

At fleet scale, a marginal SM or a bad memory cell can compute wrong-but-not-crashing math — a documented industry problem. Nothing throws an error; your loss curve just quietly diverges or your eval silently degrades. Defenses: record per-host Xid / ECC / row-remap events in lineage and drain suspect nodes; run periodic numerical parity checks against a golden reference; checksum critical tensors; and treat "which nodes touched this run" as a first-class, queryable fact so you can quarantine a suspect chip across every job it touched.

The metrics pipeline & auto-remediation

  • Collect: a per-node agent scrapes always-on counters (device-busy %, power, HBM, ECC, step time) cheaply, plus on-demand deep traces. Ship metrics to a TSDB (Prometheus / VictoriaMetrics), logs to Loki, dashboards in Grafana.
  • Cardinality guard: keep op_name / step out of metric tags — a 4,000-device job at 50k ops/step is 200M series otherwise. Bounded aggregates in the TSDB; per-op detail in columnar blobs bridged by trace-IDs.
  • Query & alert: PromQL over the fleet, e.g. detect a node whose ECC rate exceeds baseline, or a job whose MFU dropped 12% vs last week.
  • Auto-remediate: alert → cordon node → drain job to checkpoint → swap hot spare → open a repair ticket — without a human in the loop for the common cases.

Performance Profiling

Hardware counters, the roofline, bottleneck classification, and building a profiling pipeline that tells you the fix — not just the symptom.

Analogy: your car feels slow. Is the engine maxed out (compute-bound), or is it starved for fuel (memory-bound), or stuck in traffic waiting for the car ahead (latency/comms-bound), or is the fuel line the bottleneck (PCIe/input-bound)? "The car is slow" isn't actionable; "the engine is at 90% but fuel flow is at 40% of capacity" tells you exactly what to fix. Profiling turns "it's slow" into "this specific resource is the wall."

The roofline: two numbers classify any kernel

  • Arithmetic intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes moved to/from HBM (FLOP/byte). Analytic from the op's shape.
  • Ridge point = peak-FLOP/s ÷ peak-HBM-bandwidth for that chip — order of hundreds of FLOP/byte on a big GPU (a few hundred for BF16, up to ~1,000 at FP8 peak).

Plot achieved FLOP/s vs intensity. Below the ridge you're on the slanted memory roof (bandwidth-limited); at the flat compute roof you're FLOP-limited.

THE #1 TRAP: "GPU utilization" lies

nvidia-smi "GPU utilization" only means a kernel was resident during the sample — a coarse busy flag. One tiny memory-bound kernel keeps the device "90% busy" while the ALUs stall on memory. SM occupancy is also not "useful work" — a kernel can be 100% occupied and fully memory-bound. The real useful-work signal is achieved-vs-peak FLOP/s (MFU) and tensor-core-active %. Reach for MFU, not busy %.

The counters & the correlation problem

  • Hardware counters: via CUPTI (kernel timing + correlation IDs), DCGM/NVML (power, SM activity, HBM), NCCL profiler plugin (collectives); on TPU, the XLA profiler / libtpu path exposes MXU FLOP utilization. PyTorch's Kineto wraps CUPTI.
  • Two collection modes: always-on counters at <1% overhead for fleet health; on-demand deep traces (up to ~30% overhead, bounded to a few steps) for interactive RCA. Auto-arm the deep trace when always-on step-time regresses.
  • Host↔device clock correlation: accelerators run async (host enqueues, returns), so you need correlation IDs to pair a host launch with its device kernel, plus a shared reference clock (with a stated error bound) to put both on one timeline. The ID removes the matching ambiguity; the clock estimate removes the timeline ambiguity — they're separate problems.

Building the profiling pipeline (the real deliverable)

# Agent per node: cheap always-on counters + gated deep-trace sessions.
class ProfilerAgent:
    def sample(self):                       # <1% overhead, every few seconds
        push(counters=[step_time, sm_active, hbm_bw, ecc, power])
    def on_regression(self, signal):        # step time up > threshold?
        if free_hbm() > SAFETY_MARGIN:        # never OOM the job we profile
            arm_deep_trace(steps=10)         # bounded window, drop-on-full
        else:
            degrade_to_counter_only()         # fail toward blind, never toward incident

The analysis layer runs a battery of detectors (input starvation, memory-bound cluster, straggler, slow collective, low batch occupancy) and emits a ranked, plain-language list with a $ / MFU impact and a suggested fix, with the roofline and trace one click underneath for experts. Every finding carries a dollar figure via $ ≈ (ΔMFU / MFU) × accel-hours × $/accel-hr.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Probe: "A 400-GPU job runs at half throughput — is it their code or our fabric?" Crisp: "MFU first — the single 'are we using the silicon' number. Then split compute- vs comms-bound from traces; check comms/compute overlap; per-rank step-time histogram for a straggler; NCCL topology + NUMA placement; and input-pipeline starvation (device idle waiting on the dataloader). Lineage + per-rank telemetry let me attribute it to 'your code — starved input or bad overlap' vs 'our fabric — slow link or straggler host.' And the profiler must never OOM the job it profiles."

End-to-End: How a Model Graph Becomes Scheduled Kernels

The whole journey, from a few lines of Python to thousands of kernels running in lockstep across a supercomputer. Every earlier tab is one stop on this road.

Analogy: a music score becoming a live symphony. The composer writes notes (the model graph). An arranger optimizes it for the specific orchestra — merging parts, fixing keys (the compiler: fusion, layout). A conductor assigns sections to players and decides who plays when (sharding + scheduling). Then the actual performance happens, with everyone synchronized by the beat (collectives) and a stage manager swapping in a substitute if a violinist's string snaps (reliability). This tab ties the whole chapter together.

Walk the pipeline, stage by stage

Press Step to trace a training step from Python down to synchronized kernels across the fleet. Each stage names the tab where it lives.

Stage 1 / 8 Ready. A user writes a model in PyTorch/JAX and hits ‘train’.

The stages in words

  1. Author (graph): the model is a dataflow graph of ops (matmuls, attention, norms) in PyTorch/JAX.
  2. Trace & lower to IR: the compiler (XLA/MLIR) captures the graph into an intermediate representation it can analyze and transform.
  3. Optimize: operator fusion (merge memory-bound ops into one kernel to raise arithmetic intensity), layout selection, and memory planning — the Compiler tab.
  4. Shard: auto-sharding (GSPMD-style) or explicit FSDP/TP/PP spec cuts tensors across devices and inserts the right collectives — the Parallelism tab.
  5. Place & schedule: the cluster scheduler gang-schedules the workers onto a topology-aware slice (TP on NVLink, DP on DCN) — the Scheduling tab.
  6. Codegen kernels: ops become tuned GEMM/attention kernels for tensor cores / systolic arrays — the Kernel layer.
  7. Execute + synchronize: forward/backward run; gradients all-reduce, overlapped with compute — the Collectives tab.
  8. Observe + survive: telemetry, profiling, checkpointing, and failure recovery keep the run finishing — the Reliability & Profiling tabs.
THE UNIFYING IDEA

Every layer exists to close the gap between FLOPs the hardware can do and FLOPs the model actually uses (MFU). The compiler raises intensity; sharding makes the model fit; the scheduler keeps collectives on fast fabric; the runtime keeps batches full; profiling finds the wall; reliability keeps the run alive. Serving is the same pipeline with the batch dimension being live requests instead of training data. This mirrors the TPU and Cloud AI/ML Infra chapters — same physics (HBM, collectives, roofline), different entry point.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Probe: "Walk me through what happens between model.train() and gradients being applied across 1,000 chips." A strong answer names the stages above and the failure mode at each (fusion missed → memory-bound; bad sharding → collective on slow fabric; no gang → hung job; no overlap → comms-bound; no checkpoint → lost days). Showing you can traverse the whole stack and localize a slowdown to a stage is the staff-level signal.

Terminology & Acronyms

Quick reference for the key terms in this chapter.

TermMeaning
Collectives (all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter, all-to-all)Group communication primitives; e.g. all-reduce leaves every device holding the element-wise sum of all devices' arrays. All-reduce = reduce-scatter then all-gather.
CUPTICUDA Profiling Tools Interface — NVIDIA's API for per-kernel timing and hardware counters; wrapped by PyTorch's Kineto.
DCGMData Center GPU Manager — NVIDIA's fleet monitoring stack for GPU health counters (power, ECC, SM activity).
DCNData-center network — the slowest interconnect tier (Ethernet/IP); reserve it for once-per-step data-parallel all-reduce.
DP / TP / PP / EPData, tensor, pipeline, and expert parallelism — the four axes for splitting a model across chips, usually composed together.
FSDPFully Sharded Data Parallel — shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer state across ranks (equivalent to ZeRO-3), all-gathering each layer's weights on demand.
GEMMGeneral matrix multiply — the dense linear-algebra kernel at the core of transformer compute.
HBMHigh-bandwidth memory — the accelerator's on-package DRAM; its bandwidth is the ceiling on memory-bound decode.
ICI / OCSInter-Chip Interconnect — Google TPU's high-speed links between chips (the TPU analog of NVLink); an optical circuit switch (OCS) reconfigures the torus topology per job.
KV cache / paged attentionCached key/value vectors of prior tokens in HBM; paged attention allocates them in fixed-size blocks, like OS virtual memory, to cut fragmentation.
MFUModel FLOPs Utilization — achieved FLOP/s divided by the chip's peak; the guiding metric for how well the silicon is used.
MoEMixture of Experts — a model where a router sends each token to a few specialist sub-networks, driving all-to-all communication.
NCCLNVIDIA Collective Communications Library — the GPU library that implements all-reduce and the other collectives.
NVLink / NVSwitchNVIDIA's high-bandwidth intra-node GPU interconnect and its switch; keep chatty tensor-parallel collectives inside this domain.
Prefill / decodeThe two inference phases: prefill processes the whole prompt (compute-bound); decode emits one token per step (memory-bandwidth-bound).
RoCE / InfiniBandRack-level RDMA fabrics; RoCE = RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RDMA = remote direct memory access), enabling GPUDirect transfers between nodes.
Roofline / arithmetic intensityArithmetic intensity = FLOPs per byte moved from HBM; plotted on the roofline it shows whether a kernel is memory- or compute-bound (split at the ridge point).
SDCSilent data corruption — hardware computing wrong-but-not-crashing results; caught by parity checks and ECC/Xid lineage, not by an error.
SHARPScalable Hierarchical Aggregation and Reduction Protocol — NVIDIA's in-network reduction that sums gradients inside the switch.
TTFT / TPOTTime to first token (set by prefill) and time per output token (set by decode) — the two serving latency SLOs.
XLA / MLIR / HLOCompiler infrastructures that lower a model graph to optimized kernels: XLA (Accelerated Linear Algebra, via its High Level Operations / HLO IR) and MLIR (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation) do fusion, layout, and sharding.
ZeROZero Redundancy Optimizer — sharding stages (ZeRO-1/2/3) that partition optimizer state, then gradients, then parameters across data-parallel ranks.
