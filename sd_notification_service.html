Design a Notification Service

The classic fan-out delivery problem: take an event ("your order shipped", a password reset, a marketing blast) and get it to the right user on the right channel — push, email, SMS, or in-app — reliably, without spamming, and at a few thousand messages per second steady-state (tens of thousands at peak), spiking into the hundreds of thousands during broadcasts. Every number below is stated precisely so you can quote it in an interview.
Read this like a story

We start from "what even is a notification service" and build up to "deliver a 50-million-user broadcast without overwhelming a third-party gateway or double-sending." The two ideas that carry the whole design — an async queue in the middle and an idempotency (dedup) key so retries never double-send — are introduced early and reused everywhere.

1. Problem & Requirements

A notification service is a shared platform that other systems call to deliver a message to a user across one or more channels — mobile push, email, SMS, and in-app (a bell/inbox inside your own product). The caller says who, what, and how urgent; the service figures out which devices/addresses to hit, which provider to send through, whether the user even wants it, and retries until it lands.

The problem in one sentence

Given a stream of events from many producer services, deliver the right notification to the right user on the right channel, at least once, respecting the user's preferences and quiet hours, fast for the urgent ones, and without ever double-sending or spamming.

Channels & message types

Two orthogonal dimensions. First, the channel (the transport):

ChannelHow it's deliveredNotes
Mobile pushThrough a push provider (the platform's push gateway) using per-device tokensCheap, instant, but tokens expire and users can revoke; needs the OS platform's gateway
EmailSMTP or an email provider's APIRich content; slower feedback (bounces/complaints arrive async); reputation matters
SMSThrough an SMS gateway / aggregatorHigh reliability + reach, but costs real money per message → strict caps
In-appWritten to a per-user inbox store; delivered over a websocket or on next pollYou own it end-to-end; no third party; good for non-urgent activity feeds

Second, the message type, which drives priority and compliance:

Transactional

  • Triggered by a user action or a critical system event: OTP / 2FA codes, password resets, "order shipped", payment receipts, security alerts.
  • Latency-critical and must not be dropped. Users can't reasonably opt out of a security code.
  • Gets the priority lane and generous rate budgets.

Promotional / marketing

  • Bulk campaigns, digests, re-engagement nudges, "50% off this weekend".
  • Best-effort, and users can fully opt out — legally required in many regions (unsubscribe, consent).
  • Rate-capped hard, subject to quiet hours, and sent on the low-priority lane so a 50M blast never delays an OTP.

Requirements to pin down

Functional

  • Multi-channel send: one API to send push / email / SMS / in-app, plus multi-channel ("push, and if not seen in 5 min, email").
  • Templates: reusable, localized content with variable substitution — producers send data, not final strings.
  • User preferences: per-user, per-channel, per-category opt-in/opt-out; quiet hours; unsubscribe.
  • Scheduling & broadcast: send now, send later, or fan out to a large audience.
  • Tracking: expose delivery status (queued / sent / delivered / failed / opened).

Non-functional

  • High throughput: hundreds of thousands of sends/sec at peak (broadcasts).
  • At-least-once delivery with idempotency so a retry never double-sends.
  • Low latency for critical messages (OTP in seconds); promotional can lag minutes.
  • Respect preferences & quiet hours; don't spam (per-user rate caps, collapse duplicates).
  • Availability & isolation: a provider outage or a huge broadcast must not take the whole platform down.
The delivery-guarantee choice, stated up front

Exactly-once delivery across a third party you don't control is effectively impossible — the provider may accept a message and have your acknowledgment lost on the wire, so you can't tell "sent" from "maybe sent". The industry answer is at-least-once + idempotency: retry freely, but attach a dedup key so duplicates collapse to a single actual send. Saying this sentence early is a strong senior signal (see §6).

2. Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

Sizing decides queue capacity, worker fleet size, provider contracts, and how much delivery-status data you retain. Round aggressively and state every step.

Assumptions

  • 100 million registered users; 20 million daily active.
  • Average 2 devices per user → device-token table on the order of 200M tokens.
  • Total 500 million notifications/day across all channels (transactional + promotional).

Per-channel split

Apply a typical mix — push dominates because it's free and instant; SMS is among the smallest slices (tied with in-app) because it costs money:

total/day = 500,000,000 (5 × 108)
push 70% = 350,000,000
email 20% = 100,000,000
SMS 5% = 25,000,000 # smallest paid channel — metered, ~$ per message
in-app 5% = 25,000,000
# 350M + 100M + 25M + 25M = 500M ✓

QPS: average and peak

seconds/day = 86,400 ≈ 105
average QPS = 5 × 108 / 86,400 ≈ 5,800 sends/sec # ≈5,000 with the ÷10⁵ shortcut
peak QPS = average × (peak factor ≈ 5) ≈ ~29,000 sends/sec

~29k peak sends/sec is very manageable for a partitioned queue and a modest worker fleet — the interesting spikes come from broadcasts, next.

Broadcast fan-out — the real spike

A single "product launch" campaign to 50 million opted-in users is one API call that explodes into 50M individual sends. If we want it delivered within 10 minutes (600 s):

broadcast QPS = 50,000,000 / 600 ≈ 83,000 sends/sec # on TOP of the ~29k peak
# deliver in 5 min instead → 50,000,000 / 300 ≈ 166,000 sends/sec
This is the whole reason for the queue

One broadcast momentarily triples or quadruples system load. You cannot provision the synchronous path for the peak of every campaign, and you must not let it starve OTPs. The fix (see §3 and §8): the broadcast is expanded asynchronously into a queue and paced to what the providers can absorb — the spike becomes a longer, flatter tail instead of a wall.

Storage for delivery status

You log a status record per send for tracking, debugging, and compliance. Assume ~500 bytes each (ids, channel, timestamps, state):

per day = 5 × 108 × 500 B = 250 GB/day
30-day hot retention = 250 GB × 30 = 7.5 TB
device tokens = 200M × ~200 B ≈ 40 GB # fits in cache comfortably
Takeaway

The non-broadcast peak (~29k QPS, tens of GB of tokens) is small; the design pressure is entirely from burstiness (broadcasts) and reliability (retries, dedup, provider outages), not raw volume. Spend your interview time there.

3. High-Level Architecture

The backbone is a classic producer → ingestion → queue → workers → providers pipeline. Ingestion is synchronous and fast (validate, enqueue, return); everything expensive and failure-prone happens asynchronously behind a queue.

The pipeline

Synchronous, fast ingestion (validate + enqueue + return 202) up front; asynchronous per-channel workers behind a partitioned queue call the provider gateways and record delivery status.

Component responsibilities

  • Ingestion API: authenticate the producer, validate the request, compute the idempotency/dedup key, write the notification record, and enqueue. Returns 202 Accepted immediately — it does not wait for delivery.
  • Message queue: the durable buffer that decouples ingestion from sending. Partitioned for throughput and split into priority lanes so transactional traffic jumps ahead of promotional (see §8).
  • Per-channel workers: pull from the queue, resolve the user's devices/addresses, check preferences and quiet hours, render the template, and call the right provider adapter. One consumer group per channel so each scales independently.
  • Preferences / Subscriptions & Template services: the workers read these to decide whether and what to send.
  • Provider gateways: the push provider, email provider, and SMS gateway — third parties (or the OS platform) that actually deliver.
  • Delivery-status store: records state transitions and ingests async receipts/callbacks (delivered, bounced, complained) for tracking.

Why a queue in the middle

The single most important design decision

Sending is slow and failure-prone: a provider call can take hundreds of ms, time out, throttle you, or be down. If ingestion sent synchronously, the producer would block on every hiccup and a broadcast would overwhelm everything. The queue absorbs bursts (broadcast smoothing), decouples producer speed from provider speed, enables retries without blocking the caller, and gives natural backpressure. Async-behind-a-queue is the answer to almost every follow-up in this problem.

4. Data Model

Five core entities. Keep the write path (notification + status) separate from the mostly-read config (templates, preferences, tokens) so each can use the right store.

Core entities

Preference / subscriptionuser_id, category, channel, opted_in, quiet_hours, timezone, frequency_capPer (user × category × channel). Drives the "should we send?" gate and unsubscribe.

Store choices

  • Notification + delivery status: high write volume, key-value / time-series access by id or user_id, TTL'd after retention → a wide-column / NoSQL store (e.g. Cassandra-style) partitioned by user_id or notification_id.
  • Templates & preferences: low write, heavy read, needs consistency → a relational store fronted by a cache; workers read it on the hot path so cache aggressively.
  • Device tokens: ~40 GB, read on every send → cache in memory (Redis) with the source of truth in the relational/NoSQL store.
  • Dedup keys: a fast atomic store (Redis SET key NX EX ttl) or a unique constraint on the notification table (see §6).

5. Channels & Provider Integration

Every channel does the same three things — resolve destination, render content, hand off to a provider — but the providers have wildly different APIs, error models, and quirks. The design goal is to isolate that variation.

The adapter pattern (one interface, many providers)

Define a single internal ChannelSender interface; write one adapter per provider that translates the internal message into that provider's API and normalizes its response/errors back into your common vocabulary (ACCEPTED, RETRYABLE, PERMANENT_FAIL, INVALID_TOKEN). Workers program against the interface and never see provider specifics.

# common interface every channel/provider adapter implements
class ChannelSender:
    def send(self, msg, idempotency_key) -> SendResult:
        # returns ACCEPTED | RETRYABLE | PERMANENT_FAIL | INVALID_TOKEN
        ...

# a worker is provider-agnostic
result = adapter.send(msg, key=notification.dedup_key)
if   result.status == "RETRYABLE":     schedule_retry(msg)     # §6
elif result.status == "INVALID_TOKEN": prune_token(msg.token)  # stop retrying a dead device
elif result.status == "PERMANENT_FAIL": to_dead_letter(msg)    # §6
else:                                 mark_sent(msg)

Per-channel specifics

  • Push: send a device token + payload to the push provider. Handle token invalidation — when the provider says "unregistered", delete the token so you stop wasting sends. Batch where the provider allows (many tokens per call) to hit broadcast throughput.
  • Email: SMTP or a provider API. Feedback is asynchronous: bounces and spam complaints arrive later via webhooks, not in the send response — so "accepted" ≠ "delivered". Track sender reputation; suppress addresses that hard-bounce.
  • SMS: through an SMS gateway/aggregator. Costs money per message, so caps and dedup matter most here; delivery receipts arrive async; watch country-specific rules and sender IDs.
  • In-app: write to the user's inbox store and push over an open websocket if connected, else it's read on next poll/open. No third party, so it's your most reliable channel.

Provider failover & multi-provider routing

Providers have outages and regional strengths. Keep more than one provider per channel behind the adapter layer and route with a policy:

  • Primary / secondary failover: if the primary returns errors or a circuit breaker trips (see §8), fail over to a secondary provider for the same channel.
  • Health-aware routing: track each provider's recent error rate and latency; shift traffic away from a degraded one.
  • Cost/geo routing: for SMS especially, route by destination country to the cheapest reliable aggregator.
Failover meets idempotency

If you retried on provider A, timed out, then failed over to provider B, you may deliver twice. Pass the same idempotency key to the provider when it supports one, and keep your own dedup gate (§6) so a cross-provider retry still collapses to a single user-visible message.

6. Reliability: At-Least-Once, Retries, DLQ & Idempotency

This is the heart of the interview. Distributed delivery through unreliable third parties means things will fail mid-flight; the design has to make that safe rather than pretend it won't happen.

At-least-once via the queue

Workers use acknowledge-after-success semantics: a message is only removed from the queue once the send is confirmed (or permanently classified). If a worker crashes after sending but before acking, the message becomes visible again and another worker retries it — that's what guarantees at-least-once, and it's exactly why duplicates are possible and why idempotency is mandatory.

Retries: exponential backoff + jitter

On a retryable failure (timeout, 5xx, throttling), don't retry immediately in a tight loop — you'd hammer an already-struggling provider. Back off exponentially, and add jitter so many workers that failed at the same instant don't retry in a synchronized "thundering herd":

# attempt n = 1, 2, 3, ... base = 1s, factor = 2, cap = 300s
delay(n) = min(cap, base × 2(n−1)) + random_jitter
# → ~1s, 2s, 4s, 8s, 16s, 32s ... each ± jitter

if attempt > MAX_RETRIES (e.g. 5–6): send to dead-letter queue

Only retry retryable errors. A 400 invalid payload or an INVALID_TOKEN will never succeed — retrying wastes work and, for SMS, money. Classify errors into retryable vs. permanent and treat them differently.

Dead-letter queue (DLQ)

After the retry budget is exhausted (or on a permanent failure), move the message to a dead-letter queue instead of dropping it silently or blocking the main queue. The DLQ is for inspection, alerting, and manual/automated replay once the root cause is fixed. A rising DLQ rate is a key health signal.

Retry/DLQ flow: success acks and records status; a retryable error re-enqueues with exponential backoff + jitter; a permanent error or an exhausted retry budget goes to the dead-letter queue.

Idempotency — the dedup key that stops double-sends

Because delivery is at-least-once and retries/failovers can duplicate work, every notification carries an idempotency key — a deterministic id derived from the event, e.g. hash(user_id, type, event_id) (where event_id is the triggering event's unique id; fold template_id in too if one event can produce different templates for the same user). The send path checks-and-claims that key atomically before calling the provider:

# atomic claim in Redis: SET only if Not eXists, with a TTL
claimed = SET(dedup_key, notification_id, NX, EX=ttl)
if not claimed:
    return  # someone already sent (or is sending) this → skip, do NOT double-send
send_via_provider(msg, idempotency_key=dedup_key)   # pass key downstream too
At-least-once + idempotent = effectively-once

You cannot get true exactly-once through a third party, but at-least-once delivery plus an idempotency gate gives the user an effectively-once experience: retry as much as you need for reliability, and the dedup key guarantees only one message actually reaches them. Pass the key to the provider too when it supports one, so a duplicate that slips past your gate still collapses at their end.

Claim-before-send vs. commit-after-send

Claiming the key before the provider call prevents concurrent duplicates, but if the send then fails you must release or expire the claim so a legitimate retry can proceed — otherwise a transient failure permanently suppresses the notification. The TTL bounds this: either finalize the key to "sent" on success, or let it expire so retries can re-claim.

Delivery receipts & tracking

"Accepted by the provider" is not "delivered to the user." Providers send asynchronous receipts (webhooks/callbacks): delivered, bounced, failed, opened. Ingest these to advance the status state machine, prune dead tokens/addresses, feed analytics, and detect silent delivery failures. Model status as a monotonic state machine and store the provider's message id to correlate the callback.

7. Dedup, Rate Limiting & User Preferences

Reliability makes sure messages arrive; this section makes sure we don't over-send. "Don't spam the user" is as much a requirement as "don't drop the OTP."

Deduplication & collapsing

Two distinct jobs, often conflated:

  • Exact dedup (idempotency): the same logical event fired twice (a retry, a double-clicked producer) → the dedup key from §6 collapses them to one send.
  • Collapsing / coalescing: many different events that would annoy the user if sent individually (20 different people each liked your photo) → collapse into one digest ("Alice and 19 others liked your photo") within a time window.
Dedup gate: compute a deterministic key, check the dedup store; a new key is recorded and sent once, a duplicate is dropped (or folded into a digest).

Per-user rate limiting (frequency caps)

Cap how many notifications a user receives per window ("at most 5 promotional pushes/day", "at most 1 marketing email/day") to prevent fatigue and opt-outs. This is exactly the rate-limiter problem applied per user × category × channel.

Cross-reference

Reuse the Design a Rate Limiter chapter directly: a token bucket or sliding-window counter keyed on {user_id}:{category}:{channel} in Redis, checked atomically before enqueue/send. Transactional messages get generous or exempt buckets; promotional messages get tight caps. Providers also throttle you globally — a second, provider-side limiter (§8) protects them.

Quiet hours & time zones

Don't ping someone at 3 a.m. Store each user's timezone and quiet-hours window; before sending a non-critical message, if "now" in the user's local time falls in quiet hours, defer it (schedule for the window's end) rather than drop it. Critical transactional messages (security codes) bypass quiet hours.

Opt-out / unsubscribe

  • Every promotional channel needs a working unsubscribe (list-unsubscribe header for email, STOP keyword for SMS, in-app toggles) — legally required in many regions.
  • Honor it on the send path: the preferences gate rejects a message for a category/channel the user opted out of, before it reaches the provider.
  • Keep a suppression list (hard bounces, complaints, STOPs) that overrides everything — never send to a suppressed address.

Priority lanes

Separate the queue into priority classes so a 50M promotional broadcast can't delay a password-reset code. Workers drain the high-priority (transactional) lane first, or dedicate a worker pool to it. Combined with per-user caps, this gives the two key guarantees: critical is fast and promotional is bounded.

8. Scaling & Bottlenecks

At ~29k peak QPS with 83k+ broadcast spikes, the constraints are queue throughput, provider limits, and fairness — not storage.

Queue partitioning

Partition the queue (by user_id or by channel) so many workers consume in parallel — throughput scales with partition count. Partitioning by user_id also lets you preserve per-user ordering within a partition while still parallelizing across users. Keep separate topics/lanes per priority so promotional volume never head-of-line-blocks transactional.

Provider throttling & backpressure

The gateways impose their own rate limits; exceed them and they throttle or ban you. So the workers must rate-limit their own outbound calls per provider (a token bucket per provider — the rate limiter again) and respond to backpressure:

  • When a provider returns 429/throttling, slow the consumers (reduce concurrency, honor Retry-After) rather than retrying blindly.
  • Use a circuit breaker: after repeated failures, stop hammering the provider, fail over to a secondary (§5), and let the queue buffer until it recovers.
  • The queue depth is your backpressure signal — a growing backlog tells you to add workers or that a provider is the bottleneck.

Hot broadcasts

A 50M-user broadcast is expanded asynchronously: the single API call writes one broadcast record, and a fan-out job enumerates the audience and enqueues per-user messages in batches — never synchronously in the request. Then pace the drain to what the providers can absorb (§2's 83k/s becomes a controlled, flatter tail), and run it on the low-priority lane so transactional traffic is unaffected. Batch provider calls (many push tokens per request) to raise throughput.

Ordering vs. throughput

The core scaling trade-off

Strict global ordering forces serialization and kills throughput. In practice notifications rarely need global order — at most per-user order ("shipped" before "delivered"), which partitioning by user_id gives you cheaply. Accept unordered delivery across users to keep the fan-out massively parallel. If two messages truly must be ordered, key them to the same partition; don't impose ordering platform-wide.

10. Interview Framing

How to drive the conversation

  1. Clarify (1–2 min): Which channels? Transactional, promotional, or both? What delivery guarantee and latency for critical? Scale (DAU, sends/day, biggest broadcast)? Do we own the providers or integrate third parties?
  2. Estimate (§2): sends/day → per-channel split → average and peak QPS → broadcast fan-out. Land on "steady state is small; broadcasts + reliability are the hard parts."
  3. Draw the pipeline (§3): producer → ingestion (202) → queue → per-channel workers → provider adapters → status store. Emphasize the queue.
  4. Go deep on reliability (§6): at-least-once, retries with backoff+jitter, DLQ, and the idempotency/dedup key that makes it effectively-once.
  5. Don't-spam & scale (§7–§8): preferences/quiet hours, per-user rate caps (cross-ref rate limiter), priority lanes, provider throttling/backpressure, hot broadcasts.

What interviewers probe (with one-liners)

Q: How do you avoid double-sending?
Attach a deterministic idempotency/dedup key to every notification (hash(user_id, type, event_id)) and atomically claim it (Redis SET NX EX, or a unique constraint) before calling the provider — a duplicate finds the key already claimed and skips. Pass the key to the provider too so anything past your gate collapses at their end. At-least-once + idempotency = effectively-once.

Q: How do you handle a provider outage?
Retryable errors back off exponentially with jitter and re-enqueue; a circuit breaker stops hammering a down provider and fails over to a secondary; the durable queue buffers the backlog until recovery; anything exhausting its retry budget lands in the DLQ for replay. The producer never blocks because ingestion is async.

Q: How do you send a 10M-user (or 50M) broadcast without overwhelming things?
Don't expand it synchronously. Write one broadcast record, then a fan-out job enumerates the audience and enqueues per-user messages in batches on the low-priority lane. Workers pace outbound calls to each provider's rate limit and batch tokens per call, turning an 83k/s wall into a controlled tail that never delays transactional traffic.

Q: Critical vs. promotional — how do you prioritize?
Separate priority lanes in the queue: transactional drains first (or on a dedicated pool), promotional is rate-capped, subject to quiet hours, and users can fully opt out. A password reset is never stuck behind a marketing blast, and no user gets spammed.

Q: Why at-least-once and not exactly-once?
Through a third-party provider you can't distinguish "delivered" from "delivered-but-ack-lost", so exactly-once is unachievable. You choose at-least-once for reliability and add idempotency to make it effectively-once from the user's perspective.

Q: What does the queue actually buy you?
Decoupling (producer speed ≠ provider speed), burst absorption (broadcasts), retry without blocking callers, backpressure via queue depth, and failure isolation (a slow provider fills one lane, not the whole platform).

Q: How do you respect user preferences and quiet hours?
A preferences/subscriptions service is checked on the send path before the provider call: opted-out or suppressed → drop; in quiet hours (in the user's timezone) and non-critical → defer to the window's end; also enforce per-user frequency caps with a rate limiter. Critical security messages bypass quiet hours.

One-line summary

A notification service is an async fan-out pipeline: ingest fast and return, buffer in a partitioned priority queue, let per-channel workers render + check preferences + call provider adapters with retries/backoff/jitter/DLQ, and make the whole thing effectively-once with an idempotency key — while per-user caps, quiet hours, and collapsing keep it from ever spamming anyone.
