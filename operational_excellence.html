Reliability as a Feature

Shipping code is only half the job. The other half — keeping it healthy while real users depend on it — is the operational excellence discipline. This chapter is that discipline, end to end.
ANALOGY

Think of a restaurant. A junior cook can plate one beautiful dish. A head chef keeps a full dining room fed on a Friday night: the fryer catches fire, a supplier is late, two cooks call in sick — and dinner still goes out. Writing the feature is the dish. Operational excellence is running the Friday-night service. Interviews for senior roles test the second skill, because in production every night is Friday night.

Why "reliability is a feature"

Users do not experience your architecture diagram; they experience whether the button worked this time. A checkout page that is brilliant but down 1% of the time loses more revenue than a plain one that is always up. So reliability competes for engineering time exactly like any other feature — and, crucially, it can be over-built. Chasing 99.999% when users cannot tell the difference from 99.9% burns money that a new feature would have earned. The senior instinct is to make reliability a budgeted, measured trade-off, not an absolute.

Tiny worked example

A service does 10,000,000 requests/day. Compare two reliability targets over a 30-day month (300M requests):

TargetAllowed failures / monthFailed user requestsCost to reach it
99.9%0.1%300,000Good monitoring + safe deploys
99.99%0.01%30,000Multi-region, hot failover, heavy automation
99.999%0.001%3,000Full active-active, seconds-level failover, huge on-call load

Each extra "nine" cuts failures 10× but often multiplies cost. The question is never "can we hit five nines?" — it is "does the user, or the contract, actually need it?"

The operational excellence stack

Everything in this chapter fits one loop: define what "healthy" means → measure it → protect it while changing the system → respond fast when it breaks → learn without blame → automate the lesson away.

DEV vs SRE MINDSET

A developer optimizes for change velocity (ship the feature). An SRE optimizes for stability (don't break prod). Left unchecked they fight forever. The error budget (next section) is the treaty that lets both win: change fast while you have budget, slow down when you don't.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

When an interviewer asks "how would you make this reliable?", do not jump to "add more replicas." Say: "First I'd define the reliability target as an SLO tied to the user journey, derive an error budget, and let that budget decide how much we invest — because reliability is a feature with diminishing returns, not an absolute." That single sentence signals L5+ maturity.

SLI / SLO / SLA & Error Budgets

The measurement backbone. An SLI is what you measure, an SLO is the target, an SLA is the contract — and the error budget is the number that turns reliability into a spendable resource.
ANALOGY

An error budget is a monthly data allowance on a phone plan. You get a fixed amount of "allowed unreliability" each month. Spend it slowly and you can keep shipping risky new things. Blow through it early — a bad launch, an outage — and you get throttled: no new features until next cycle, only reliability work. It converts a vague argument ("are we stable enough to ship?") into arithmetic.

The three terms, precisely

TermWhat it isExampleAudience
SLI — IndicatorA measured ratio of good events to total events% of requests served < 300ms without a 5xx (server-error response)Engineers
SLO — ObjectiveInternal target for the SLI over a window99.9% over a rolling 28 daysEngineering + product
SLA — AgreementExternal contract with penalties99.5% or the customer gets creditsLegal + customers

Golden rule: SLO is stricter than SLA. If you promise customers 99.5% but internally target 99.9%, you get warned and fix things long before you owe anyone a refund.

Error-budget math — work it out loud

The error budget is simply 100% − SLO, expressed as allowed downtime (or allowed bad requests) over the window. Memorize the anchor: 99.9% over 30 days ≈ 43 minutes.

Window   = 30 days = 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes
SLO      = 99.9%   => budget = 0.1% = 0.001
Budget   = 0.001 × 43,200 = 43.2 minutes of "allowed down" per month

Same window, other targets:
  99%     -> 1.0%   -> 432 min   (~7.2 hours)
  99.9%   -> 0.1%   -> 43.2 min
  99.95%  -> 0.05%  -> 21.6 min
  99.99%  -> 0.01%  -> 4.32 min
  99.999% -> 0.001% -> 25.9 seconds  (!)

Request-based works the same way. At 300M requests/month and a 99.9% success SLO, the budget is 0.001 × 300,000,000 = 300,000 failed requests. A bad deploy that errors for 6 minutes at 10k req/min burns 60,000 — a fifth of the month's budget in one release.

How the budget gates releases

This is the key mechanism. The budget is a policy, automatically enforced:

  • Budget healthy (>25% left): ship freely, take risks, run experiments.
  • Budget low (<25%): slow down — extra review, smaller canaries, no risky launches.
  • Budget exhausted (0): release freeze. Only reliability fixes ship until the rolling window earns budget back. The team that caused the burn now owns the fix — incentives aligned.

A refinement is the burn rate alert: instead of waiting for the budget to hit zero, alert when it is draining too fast. A burn rate of 1 means you will exactly exhaust the budget at the end of the window; a burn rate of 14.4 over 1 hour means you would burn a 30-day budget in ~2 days — page immediately.

Watch the budget burn (replayable)

Step through a month. Each step is a week of normal deploys plus the occasional incident eating into the 43.2-minute budget. Watch the gate flip from SHIP to SLOW to FREEZE.

COMMON MISTAKE

Setting the SLO to 100%. That means zero error budget — every deploy, every maintenance window, every dependency hiccup is a "violation." It makes the whole system brittle and stops all change. The point of an SLO is to define how much unreliability is acceptable, and the honest answer is never zero.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Expect: "Your SLO is 99.9% monthly — how much downtime is that, and what happens when you exceed it?" Answer crisply: "~43 minutes a month. Exceed it and we freeze feature releases and shift to reliability work until the rolling window recovers budget. Before that, a burn-rate alert pages us if we're draining too fast to survive the month." That covers the math, the gate, and the early-warning — the full picture.

Incident Response

When production breaks, the difference between a 5-minute blip and a 5-hour outage is process, not heroics. A structured response makes chaos boring.
ANALOGY

Incident response is the hospital emergency room. Patients are triaged by severity (a heart attack jumps the queue over a sprained wrist). One doctor runs the case — they don't personally do every scan, they coordinate. Someone keeps the family updated so the team isn't interrupted. That structure is exactly the Incident Commander model, and it exists so nobody freezes or trips over each other when adrenaline is high.

Severity levels — triage first

Before doing anything, classify the blast radius. Severity drives who gets paged and how loudly.

SevMeaningExampleResponse
SEV1Critical, business-wide outageCheckout down for all users; data lossAll-hands, IC + comms, exec updates
SEV2Major degradation, subset of usersOne region down; core feature brokenOn-call + IC, active bridge
SEV3Minor / partial, workaround existsElevated latency; non-critical feature failingOn-call handles in hours
SEV4/5Cosmetic or low-impactTypo in UI; noisy but harmless logNormal ticket queue

The roles — separate coordinating from fixing

Incident Commander (IC)

  • Owns the incident, not the fix
  • Decides, delegates, tracks actions
  • Never head-down in a terminal
  • Declares severity & resolution

Ops / Subject-matter leads

  • The hands actually mitigating
  • Propose fixes to the IC
  • Own one workstream each
  • Report status, don't freelance

Comms lead / Scribe

  • Updates stakeholders on a cadence
  • Shields responders from questions
  • Keeps a timestamped log
  • Posts status-page updates
WHY SEPARATE THE IC

If the person fixing the database is also fielding "is it fixed yet?" from five VPs, both jobs suffer. The IC absorbs coordination so responders can focus. In small incidents one person wears all hats — but the roles still exist, and naming them out loud prevents the classic "everyone assumed someone else was handling it."

The incident lifecycle & the metrics that grade it

  • MTTD — Mean Time To Detect: fault to alert. Bad monitoring shows up here.
  • MTTA — Mean Time To Acknowledge: alert to a human on it. Bad paging/rotations show up here.
  • MTTR — Mean Time To Recover/Resolve: the headline number — total user pain. Runbooks and rollback tooling shrink it.
  • MTBF — Mean Time Between Failures: how often it breaks at all.

Worked example

Fault at 02:00. Alert fires 02:08 (MTTD = 8 min). On-call acks 02:11 (MTTA = 3 min). Rolled back and healthy 02:26 (MTTR = 26 min). If this SLO allows 43 min/month, this single incident spent 26 of it — over half. That is why cutting MTTD (better alerts) and MTTR (one-click rollback) directly buys back error budget.

Mitigate first, diagnose later

The cardinal rule under fire: stop the bleeding before you understand the wound. If a deploy 10 minutes ago correlates with the spike, roll it back now — don't debug the root cause with users hurting. Root cause is for the postmortem; mitigation is for the incident. Common fast mitigations: roll back the last deploy, flip a feature flag off, fail over to a healthy region, shed load, or scale out.

TRAP

"Let me just find the root cause first." During a SEV1 that is the wrong instinct — every minute of debugging is a minute of user pain and burned budget. Restore service by any safe means (rollback, flag, failover), then investigate on a healthy system. Diagnosing in prod under pressure also risks a second outage.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

"Your service is throwing 500s right now — walk me through the first 10 minutes." Structure the answer: (1) declare severity, (2) assign IC + comms, (3) mitigate fast — check recent deploys and roll back / flag off, (4) communicate on a cadence, (5) only then root-cause, (6) schedule a blameless postmortem. Leading with "I'd look at the logs" reads as junior; leading with "declare, mitigate, communicate" reads as senior.

On-Call & Healthy Rotations

On-call is how someone is always ready to respond. Done well it's sustainable and educational; done badly it burns out your best engineers and they quit.
ANALOGY

On-call is a lifeguard shift. Most of the time you watch a calm pool; occasionally you sprint. The job is only humane if shifts are a reasonable length, you're not the sole guard for a giant beach, and the alarm only sounds for someone actually drowning — not for every splash. A pool that blares its alarm at every ripple gets a lifeguard who ignores it, and that's when someone drowns.

What makes a rotation healthy

  • Enough people: a rule of thumb is a minimum of 6–8 engineers per rotation so any individual is on-call roughly one week in 6–8, and follow-the-sun spreads night pages across time zones.
  • Bounded load: Google's SRE guidance caps on-call at handling ≤2 significant incidents per shift — more than that and the human can't do them justice. If pages exceed that, the fix is engineering (kill the noise), not "toughen up."
  • Comp & recovery: compensate on-call time and let someone who was up at 3am rest the next day. Reliability is a marathon.
  • Primary + secondary: a backup who is paged if the primary doesn't ack within, say, 5 minutes — and an escalation path beyond that.
  • You build it, you run it: the team that writes the service is on-call for it. Nothing improves reliability faster than the authors feeling their own 3am pages.

Every page must be actionable

The fastest way to destroy a rotation is alert fatigue: pages that are noise, not signal. If an alert fires and the on-call's correct action is "acknowledge and ignore," that alert is broken. The test for a page: a human must need to do something, right now, that automation can't.

Signal typeRoute toWhy
User-facing SLO burning fastPage (wake someone)Real pain, needs a human now
Degradation, has hours of budgetTicketFix in business hours
Informational / trendDashboardLook at it when investigating
WATCH

Alert on symptoms (users see errors / latency), not causes (CPU is 80%). High CPU that isn't hurting anyone is not a reason to wake a human. Symptom-based, SLO-linked alerting is what keeps the page count — and the resignation count — low.

The handoff

Ending a shift without a clean handoff is how context gets lost. A good handoff covers: ongoing incidents, flaky alerts to expect, changes in flight, and anything the next person should watch. Ten minutes here saves an hour later.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

"Your on-call is getting paged 20 times a night — what do you do?" Wrong answer: build a bigger rotation to absorb it. Right answer: "That's an alerting problem. I'd audit every alert against 'is it actionable and user-impacting?', delete or downgrade the noise to tickets/dashboards, tune thresholds to symptoms, and track page volume as a first-class metric. Then, if load is still high, invest in automation and rotation size." It shows you treat operational load as an engineering problem.

Blameless Postmortems

The document that turns an outage into a permanent lesson. "Blameless" is the whole point: fix the system, never the person.
ANALOGY

Aviation. When a plane has a near-miss, investigators don't fire the pilot — they figure out why the system let a trained pilot make that error, then change the checklist, the cockpit layout, or the training. That's why flying is astonishingly safe. Blameless postmortems import that culture: humans will always make mistakes, so the fix is a system that tolerates them, not a search for someone to punish.

Why blameless is non-negotiable

If people get blamed, they hide mistakes. If they hide mistakes, you never learn the real cause, and it happens again. Blamelessness isn't about being nice — it's the only way to get honest data. The framing shifts from "who screwed up?" to "what about our system made this failure possible and easy?"

HUMAN-ERROR REFRAME

"The engineer ran the wrong command" is never a root cause — it's a starting point. Ask: why did a wrong command have no confirmation prompt? Why did it hit prod, not staging? Why was there no undo? The person is the last, most visible link in a chain of missing guardrails. Fix the chain.

The five whys — drill past the symptom

Symptom: Checkout returned 500s for 26 minutes.
  Why? The service ran out of database connections.
  Why? A new query wasn't releasing connections to the pool.
  Why? The code path missed a close() in an error branch.
  Why? Code review didn't catch it & no test covered that branch.
  Why? We have no lint rule or integration test for pool leaks.
Root causes (systemic): missing pool-leak test + no connection-count alert.
Action items: add leak test to CI; alert on pool utilization >85%.

A short sample postmortem

PMPostmortem: Checkout outage, 2024-06-14SEV1▶

Summary

For 26 minutes (02:00–02:26 UTC) ~40% of checkout requests returned HTTP 500. Root cause: a deploy introduced a connection-pool leak that exhausted the database connection pool under load. Mitigated by rollback.

Impact

~62,000 failed checkouts; est. revenue impact ~$48k; 26 min of a 43-min monthly error budget consumed (60%).

Timeline (UTC)

  • 01:52 — Deploy v412 rolled to 100% (canary window was too short to surface the leak).
  • 02:00 — Pool saturates under peak traffic; 500s begin.
  • 02:08 — SLO burn-rate alert pages on-call (MTTD 8 min).
  • 02:11 — On-call acks, declares SEV1, assumes IC (MTTA 3 min).
  • 02:19 — Recent deploy identified as prime suspect; rollback started.
  • 02:26 — v411 fully restored; errors clear (MTTR 26 min).

Root cause

An error branch in the new pricing query never returned its connection. Under normal load the leak was slow; at 02:00 peak it drained the pool in minutes. Review and CI had no coverage for pool leaks.

What went well / poorly

Well: burn-rate alert fired fast; rollback tooling was one command. Poorly: canary bake time (2 min) was far too short to expose a load-dependent leak.

ACTION-ITEM DISCIPLINE

A postmortem with no owned, tracked, dated action items is theater. The value isn't the document — it's the follow-through that makes the same failure impossible. Review open postmortem actions like any other backlog.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

"How do you run a postmortem?" Hit three beats: (1) blameless — systems, not people, because blame hides data; (2) structured — summary, impact, timeline with MTTD/MTTR, root cause via five-whys; (3) actionable — owned, tracked items that are all systemic. Bonus: mention that "human error" is a prompt to find the missing guardrail, never a conclusion.

Runbooks & Automation

Captured knowledge that lets a half-asleep on-call engineer do the right thing at 3am — and the ladder that turns that knowledge into code.
ANALOGY

A runbook is the pre-flight checklist. Pilots are experts, yet they still read a printed list before every takeoff, because memory fails under stress and the cost of a missed step is catastrophic. A runbook does the same for an incident: it's the sequence of checks and actions for a known failure, written when everyone was calm, for use when nobody is.

What a good runbook contains

  • When to use it: the exact alert/symptom that leads here.
  • How to confirm: the dashboard or query that verifies the problem.
  • How to mitigate: the concrete commands — rollback, failover, flag flip — copy-pasteable.
  • How to escalate: who to page if the steps don't work.
  • Links: the dashboard, the related postmortem, the architecture doc.

Every actionable alert should link to its runbook. A page that says "latency high" with no runbook forces the on-call to reinvent the response at the worst possible time.

The automation ladder

Runbooks are step one. The senior move is to climb the ladder from documented toward automatic — each rung removes human toil and error.

Example climb: an on-call used to manually restart a stuck worker (rung 1). Someone wrote it down (rung 2), then scripted it (rung 3), then wired a "restart worker" chatops command (rung 4 — chatops = trigger it straight from a chat message), then finally a health check auto-restarts on failure and only pages if it recurs 3× (rung 5). The 3am page disappears.

AUTOMATION CAUTION

Automation that acts without limits can amplify a mistake to catastrophe — an auto-remediation that reboots "unhealthy" hosts can reboot the whole fleet during a monitoring glitch. Give automation guardrails: rate limits, blast-radius caps, and a human circuit-breaker for anything destructive.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Show you think in the ladder. "For a recurring failure I'd first write a runbook so any on-call can handle it, then automate the runbook into a script, then a one-click action, and ultimately self-healing — with guardrails so the automation can't run away." That progression signals you reduce operational load systematically, not just react.

Toil & How to Reduce It

Toil is the repetitive, manual, automatable work that scales with your system but adds no lasting value. Left unchecked it eats the team alive.
ANALOGY

Toil is doing the dishes by hand for a restaurant that keeps adding tables. Each plate is easy, but the work grows with the business and never ends. Buying a dishwasher (automation) costs a day now and frees an hour every night forever. A kitchen that never buys the dishwasher drowns as it grows — that's a system that never invests against toil.

What counts as toil (the checklist)

Toil has a precise definition. Work is toil if it is:

  • Manual — a human does it by hand.
  • Repetitive — done again and again, not one-off.
  • Automatable — a machine could do it.
  • Tactical / reactive — interrupt-driven, not strategic.
  • No enduring value — the service is no better after than before.
  • Scales linearly with the system — 2× traffic means 2× the work.

Manually approving each deploy, restarting a service by hand, copy-pasting a metric into a weekly report, provisioning a user account by ticket — all toil. Note: not all manual work is toil. Design work, a genuinely one-off migration, and postmortems create lasting value.

Cap it, then attack it

Google's SRE model caps toil at ~50% of an SRE's time; the rest must be engineering that reduces future toil. Above the cap, the team is a "human ops" service that can never get ahead. The mechanism: measure toil (track it like any workload), set the cap, and when it's exceeded, redirect work to automation instead of hiring more people to shovel.

Worked example — when does automating pay off?

Task: manual cert rotation, done 2×/month, 45 min each.
Toil per year   = 2 × 12 × 45 min = 1,080 min ≈ 18 hours/year.
Automation cost = ~3 days to build & test ≈ 24 hours, once.
Payback         = 24 / 18 ≈ 1.3 years... borderline on time alone.
But add: eliminates 3am human error, frees focus, scales to N services.
=> Automate, especially since it scales across every service.

The senior nuance: don't automate purely on hours saved — weigh error reduction, on-call relief, and how the toil grows. A task that's 18 hours today but doubles yearly is worth automating even if today's math is marginal.

TRAP

"We're too busy with ops to automate the ops." That's the toil death spiral — the busier you are with manual work, the less time you have to eliminate it, so it grows. Breaking out requires protecting engineering time (the 50% cap is a rule that forces you to make time for it) even while the fire feels urgent.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Define toil crisply (manual, repetitive, automatable, no lasting value, scales with the system), then show the discipline: "I'd measure it, cap it around 50%, and when we breach the cap, treat that as a signal to invest in automation — not to hire more people to do the same manual work." Naming the 50% cap and the death-spiral shows real familiarity.

Capacity Planning, Load Shedding & Backpressure

Making sure you have enough resources for expected demand — and degrading gracefully instead of collapsing when demand exceeds supply.
ANALOGY

A restaurant on a holiday. Capacity planning is staffing and stocking for the expected rush plus a safety margin. Load shedding is the host politely turning away walk-ins once the kitchen is full — serving 100 diners well beats seating 200 and giving everyone cold, late food. Backpressure is the kitchen telling the front to stop sending orders until it catches up. All three keep a full house from becoming a meltdown.

Capacity planning

The goal: enough capacity for peak demand plus headroom, without paying for idle machines all year. Inputs are your traffic forecast, per-request cost, and a safety margin for spikes and failures.

Worked example (with Little's Law)

Little's Law:  concurrency L = arrival rate λ × latency W
Peak traffic  λ = 10,000 req/s,  avg latency W = 0.2 s
=> in-flight  L = 10,000 × 0.2 = 2,000 concurrent requests
One server handles ~200 concurrent => 2,000 / 200 = 10 servers at peak.
Add N+2 redundancy (2 spare servers beyond peak need) + 30% headroom => ~15 servers.
Provision for PEAK (plus margin), forecast from historical peaks, not averages.

Two dials: vertical (bigger machines — simple, has a ceiling) and horizontal (more machines — scales far, needs statelessness). Autoscaling adjusts horizontally to real-time demand, but it isn't instant — scale-up has a lag (boot + warm-up), so you still need buffer for sudden spikes.

When demand exceeds capacity: degrade, don't die

No amount of planning prevents the surprise 10× spike. The senior skill is choosing what happens at the limit. The failure mode to avoid is congestion collapse: as load climbs, throughput actually drops because the system spends all its time on retries, context-switching, and timeouts — a full parking lot where nobody can move.

Load shedding

  • Reject some requests early to protect the rest
  • Drop low-priority traffic first (e.g. batch before checkout)
  • Fail fast with a clear 429/503 (rate-limit / unavailable status), don't queue forever
  • Serving 80% well beats serving 100% not at all

Backpressure

  • Signal upstream to slow down sending
  • Bounded queues: when full, producers block or get rejected
  • Propagates the limit up the chain instead of hiding it
  • Prevents an unbounded queue eating all memory

Related guards: rate limiting (cap per-client requests), circuit breakers (stop calling a failing dependency so you don't pile on), and graceful degradation (serve a cached or reduced result instead of an error — e.g. show stale recommendations rather than a blank page).

WATCH

Unbounded queues are a trap. "We'll just queue the overflow" feels safe but a queue with no limit grows until it exhausts memory and crashes — and every request in it may have already timed out on the client, so you do work nobody is waiting for. Bound every queue, and shed or apply backpressure when it fills.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

"Traffic spikes 5× your capacity — what happens?" Weak answer: "autoscale." Strong answer: "Autoscaling helps but lags, so I design for graceful degradation: rate-limit and shed low-priority load early, apply backpressure with bounded queues, use circuit breakers on dependencies, and serve degraded results over errors. The goal is to keep the core journey healthy for as many users as possible instead of collapsing for everyone." Name congestion collapse as the thing you're preventing.

Deployment Safety

Most outages are self-inflicted — a bad deploy. Safe-deploy techniques limit the blast radius of a bad change and make undo instant.
ANALOGY

Releasing to all users at once is cooking a new recipe for 500 wedding guests without tasting it. A canary release is tasting one spoonful first: serve the new dish to 1% of guests, watch for anyone getting sick, and only then serve the room. If the spoonful is bad, 1% got a slightly off bite — not 500 people with food poisoning.

The safe-deploy toolkit

TechniqueHow it worksRollbackBest for
CanarySend a small % of traffic to the new version, watch metrics, ramp upRoute traffic back to old versionCatching bad releases early with tiny blast radius
Blue-GreenTwo full environments; switch all traffic from blue to green at onceFlip back to blue instantlyFast, atomic cutover + instant rollback
RollingReplace instances in batchesRoll batches backResource-efficient, no double infra
Feature flagShip code dark, toggle behavior at runtime per user/%Flip the flag off — no redeployDecoupling deploy from release; instant kill switch

Progressive rollout ties these together: 1% → 5% → 25% → 50% → 100%, with an automated bake at each stage where you watch SLIs (error rate, latency). If a stage breaches the threshold, halt and auto-rollback. Feature flags decouple deploy (code is on the servers) from release (behavior is on for users) — so you can deploy at noon calmly and release behind a flag, and kill a bad feature without a redeploy.

Watch a canary advance — then roll back (replayable)

Step through a progressive rollout. The canary ramps while error rate stays healthy; at 25% a spike breaches the SLO and the system auto-rolls-back, protecting the other 75% of users.

DEPLOY HYGIENE

Every deploy should be reversible and observable. Reversible: you can roll back in one action (which is why blue-green and flags are loved). Observable: the canary is judged by real SLIs, not a human eyeballing it. And beware database migrations — they're often not trivially reversible, so use expand/contract (backward-compatible) schema changes so old and new code coexist during the rollout.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

"How do you deploy safely?" Cover blast radius and undo: "Progressive canary — ramp 1→100% with automated SLI checks and auto-rollback — so a bad release hits 1% of users, not 100%. I decouple deploy from release with feature flags for an instant kill switch, and keep schema changes backward-compatible so rollback is always safe." That names the two properties graders want: limited blast radius and instant, safe rollback.

Disaster Recovery, Backups & RTO/RPO

Planning for the big, rare failures — a region down, a corrupted database, a bad delete — and defining, in numbers, how fast you recover and how much data you can lose.
ANALOGY

DR is insurance and fire drills for your data. Backups are the insurance policy. But an untested backup is like an insurance policy you never read — you find out it doesn't cover the fire only when the house is burning. So the drill (restore testing) matters as much as the policy (the backup itself).

The two numbers that define DR: RTO & RPO

RTO — Recovery Time Objective

  • How long can you be down?
  • "Back online within 1 hour"
  • Drives failover speed & automation
  • Answer to: how fast must we recover?

RPO — Recovery Point Objective

  • How much data can you lose?
  • "At most 5 minutes of writes"
  • Drives backup / replication frequency
  • Answer to: how far back is the last good copy?

Worked example

Disaster: primary database is corrupted at 14:00.
RPO = 5 min  => last good copy must be no older than 13:55
              => need continuous replication or ≤5-min backups.
RTO = 1 hour => must be serving again by 15:00
              => need automated failover to a standby, not a manual
                 "restore from S3" that takes 4 hours.

Tighter targets cost more:
  RPO 24h, RTO 24h  -> nightly backup, restore by hand      (cheap)
  RPO 5m,  RTO 1h   -> continuous replication + warm standby (mid)
  RPO ~0,  RTO ~0   -> active-active multi-region            (expensive)

Set these from business impact, not ambition. A billing ledger may demand RPO near zero (losing a payment is unacceptable); a recommendations cache can tolerate hours (rebuild it). Different data classes get different targets.

Backup discipline — the 3-2-1 rule

  • 3 copies of the data, 2 different media/storage types, 1 off-site (different region/provider).
  • Test restores regularly. A backup you've never restored is a hope, not a plan. Schedule game-day restore drills.
  • Protect against logical errors, not just hardware. Replication faithfully copies a bad DROP TABLE to every replica instantly — only point-in-time backups let you recover from a bad delete.
  • Guard the backups themselves — immutable/versioned storage so ransomware or a bad actor can't delete your recovery path.

TRAP

"We have backups, so we're covered." The two classic failures: (1) the backup was never restore-tested and is corrupt/incomplete; (2) replication is mistaken for backup — it protects against hardware loss but faithfully replicates a bad delete or corruption everywhere. You need point-in-time backups and tested restores, not just replicas.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

"How would you make this survive a region outage?" Lead with the two numbers: "First I'd pin the RTO and RPO from business impact — say RTO 1h, RPO 5min — because those decide the architecture. That points to continuous cross-region replication plus automated failover, not nightly backups. I'd also test failover regularly, because an untested DR plan is a guess." Naming RTO/RPO up front is what marks a senior answer.

Chaos Engineering

Deliberately injecting failure into production-like systems to find weaknesses before they find you. You don't rise to the occasion; you fall to your level of preparation.
ANALOGY

A fire drill. You don't wait for a real fire to discover the exits are locked and nobody knows the assembly point. You start a controlled, safe "fire" on a Tuesday afternoon so that when a real one comes, the response is muscle memory. Chaos engineering runs fire drills on your infrastructure: kill a server on purpose, in daylight, with everyone watching, so you learn while it's cheap.

The method — it's a science experiment, not vandalism

  1. Define steady state: a metric that means "healthy" (e.g. checkout success ≥ 99%).
  2. Hypothesize: "If we kill one payment node, steady state holds because traffic reroutes."
  3. Inject a real but bounded failure: terminate a node, add latency, drop a dependency — with a small blast radius.
  4. Measure & compare: did steady state hold? If not, you found a weakness before a customer did.
  5. Have a blast radius & a stop button: start tiny (one host, off-peak), and be able to abort instantly.

The famous origin is Netflix's Chaos Monkey, which randomly kills production instances to force engineers to build services that survive instance loss by default. The philosophy: if failure is constant and expected, you're forced to design for it — you can't ship something fragile.

What to inject

Resource

  • Kill instances / pods
  • CPU / memory pressure
  • Disk fills up

Network

  • Latency injection
  • Packet loss
  • Dependency unreachable

State / region

  • Zone / region outage
  • Clock skew
  • Dependency returns errors
SAFETY FIRST

Chaos engineering is controlled, not reckless. Prerequisites: good observability (so you can see the impact), a small blast radius, an abort switch, and ideally run in off-peak windows first. Injecting failure into a system you can't observe or stop isn't chaos engineering — it's just an outage you caused.

INTERVIEW ANGLE

Frame it as a disciplined experiment, not "break stuff." Say: "I'd define a steady-state SLI, hypothesize that the system tolerates a specific failure, inject that failure with a bounded blast radius and an abort switch, and verify the hypothesis. It surfaces hidden coupling — a 'non-critical' dependency that actually takes down checkout — while it's cheap to fix." Mentioning steady-state + blast radius separates you from people who just name-drop Chaos Monkey.

The Observability Loop

Every discipline in this chapter depends on one thing: being able to see what your system is doing. Operational excellence and observability are two halves of one loop.
ANALOGY

Observability is the instrument panel; operational excellence is flying the plane well. You can't hold altitude if the altimeter is broken, and a perfect panel is useless if the pilot ignores it. This chapter is the flying; the Observability & Dashboards chapter is the instruments. They only work together.

Where each discipline consumes observability

Incident detection (MTTD)Symptom-based alerts on the golden signals
Incident diagnosis (MTTR)Dashboards, distributed traces, structured logs
Canary / progressive rolloutReal-time SLIs to auto-judge each stage
Capacity planningHistorical traffic & saturation metrics
PostmortemsThe timestamped log & metric history to reconstruct the timeline
Chaos experimentsThe steady-state metric to know if the hypothesis held

The unified loop

Observe what's happening → detect and respond to problems → learn from them → improve and automate → which changes what you observe next. Operational excellence is the practice of running this loop deliberately, forever. A team that closes the loop gets steadily more reliable with less effort; a team that skips a step — no postmortems, no automation, blind spots in monitoring — keeps re-fighting the same fire.

"Operational excellence and observability are one system: I can't run an error budget without SLIs, can't hit a low MTTR without traces and dashboards, and can't auto-judge a canary without real-time metrics. I'd invest in observability first, because every reliability practice downstream is only as good as the signals feeding it."
INTERVIEW ANGLE

Tie it together at the end of a reliability discussion: "All of this — budgets, incident response, safe deploys, chaos — runs on observability. It's the shared foundation." Showing you see the whole loop, not isolated tactics, is exactly the systems-thinking senior interviews are screening for.
