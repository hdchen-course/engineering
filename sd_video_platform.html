Design a Video Platform (video-sharing / short-video-style)

A large video-sharing platform, end-to-end: accept an upload, transcode it into a ladder of renditions, stream it to a global audience with adaptive bitrate over a CDN, and drive a recommendation feed. The system that stresses every axis at once — huge storage, tens of terabits per second of egress, a massive batch-compute pipeline, and an extreme read:write skew. Every number below is stated with its formula and re-checked so you can quote it in an interview.
Read this like a story

We start from "what does the product have to do," size it so you know whether you need one machine or a planet-scale fleet, then walk the request from an uploader's browser all the way to a viewer's player. The two core challenges — the transcode pipeline and adaptive-bitrate delivery — each get their own section with a diagram and the math. The feed/recommender and the trade-off framing close it out.

1. Problem & Requirements

Design the backend for a platform where users upload videos and a global audience watches them. This covers the long-form world (a large video-sharing platform) and the short-form world (a short-video app) — the ingest/transcode/delivery spine is the same; the feed differs. We must clarify scope before drawing a single box.

Functional requirements

  • Upload: a creator uploads a video file (possibly gigabytes), reliably, resumable over a flaky mobile link.
  • Transcode / process: convert the one raw upload into multiple resolutions and bitrates (a "rendition ladder") and package it for streaming.
  • Watch / stream globally: any viewer, anywhere, can play any video with low startup latency and no buffering, adapting quality to their bandwidth.
  • Search & metadata: find videos by title/tags; show title, description, thumbnail, duration, view count.
  • Recommend / feed: a home or For-You feed that decides what to show next.
  • Engagement: likes, comments, view counts, watch history.

Non-functional requirements

  • High availability for playback — watching must almost never fail (uploading can tolerate more).
  • Low startup latency — time-to-first-frame in the low hundreds of milliseconds; smooth playback without stalls.
  • Massive read:write skew — reads (views) dwarf writes (uploads) by roughly two orders of magnitude, so the whole design optimizes the read path.
  • Huge storage & bandwidth — petabytes/day of new content and tens of terabits/second of egress; cost control is a first-class concern.
  • Durability — an uploaded video, once accepted, must never be lost.
Frame the asymmetry out loud

The single most important sentence you can say early: "This is an extremely read-heavy system — a video is written (uploaded + transcoded) once and read (streamed) millions of times — so I'll spend most of the design budget on the delivery/CDN path and treat ingest as a throughput-oriented batch pipeline." That framing drives every later decision.

2. Estimation & Scale

All figures are illustrative but internally consistent — state your assumptions, then compute. I use the "÷105" shortcut (a day ≈ 86,400 s ≈ 105 s) for QPS.

Daily active viewers (DAU)500,000,000 (500 M)
Average watch time / viewer / day30 min = 1,800 s

Upload throughput (write QPS)

uploads/sec (avg) = 10,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 116 uploads/s
peak (× 5) ≈ ~580 uploads/s # modest write rate — the pipeline behind it is the hard part

Raw ingest storage

raw size / video = 12 Mbps × 300 s ÷ 8 = 3,600 Mb ÷ 8 = 450 MB
raw ingest / day = 10,000,000 × 450 MB = 4.5 × 109 MB = 4.5 PB/day # raw is transient — deleted or cold-tiered after transcode

Transcode fan-out → durable rendition storage

Each raw upload fans into a ladder of 5 video renditions. Take this bitrate ladder (verified sum):

ladder = 240p 0.4 + 360p 0.8 + 480p 1.4 + 720p 2.8 + 1080p 4.6 = 10 Mbps total
all-renditions size / video = 10 Mbps × 300 s ÷ 8 = 3,000 Mb ÷ 8 = 375 MB
rendition storage / day = 10,000,000 × 375 MB = 3.75 × 109 MB = 3.75 PB/day
rendition storage / year = 3.75 PB × 365 = ≈ 1,369 PB ≈ 1.37 EB/year

We keep the packaged renditions as the durable copy and discard (or cold-archive) the raw once transcoding succeeds — otherwise we'd pay for both. Even so, storage grows by ~1.4 exabytes/year: tiering and expiry are mandatory (§9, §10).

Transcode compute fan-out (the batch fleet)

Assume a worker encodes one rendition at ~2× real-time, so a 300 s video costs 150 CPU-s per rendition, and the 5-rung ladder costs 5 × 150 = 750 CPU-s of work per upload.

avg transcode load = 116 uploads/s × 750 CPU-s/upload = 87,000 CPU-cores busy (avg)
peak (× 5) ≈ ~435,000 cores # why it must be an autoscaling batch fleet, and why GPUs/ASIC encoders matter
Segments, too

Each rendition is chopped into ~6 s streaming segments: 300 s ÷ 6 s = 50 segments/rendition, so one 5-min upload becomes 50 × 5 = 250 media segments plus manifests. That's the unit the CDN caches and the player fetches (§6).

Watch throughput & egress bandwidth (the dominant cost)

bytes delivered / day = DAU × watch_seconds × bitrate
  = 5 × 108 × 1,800 s × 2.5 Mbps
  = (5 × 108 × 1,800) × 2.5 × 106 bits = 9 × 1011 × 2.5 × 106 = 2.25 × 1018 bits/day
  ÷ 8 = 2.81 × 1017 bytes/day ≈ 281 PB/day egress
avg egress bitrate = 2.25 × 1018 / 86,400 = 2.60 × 1013 bps = ≈ 26 Tbps (avg)
peak (× 3, prime time) ≈ ~78 Tbps

Tens of terabits per second is impossible to serve from a handful of origins — it is the reason a CDN exists (§6, §9). Egress bandwidth, not storage, is usually the largest single cost line.

Read:write ratio

videos watched / viewer / day = 1,800 s ÷ 300 s = 6
views / day = 500,000,000 × 6 = 3 × 109 views/day
read:write = 3 × 109 views ÷ 107 uploads = ≈ 300 : 1

CDN hot set (working set to cache)

Views follow a power law — a small slice of the catalog serves most traffic. Assume ~1 M "hot" videos account for the overwhelming majority of views:

hot working set = 1,000,000 videos × 375 MB (all renditions) = 3.75 × 108 MB = ≈ 375 TB
if edge hit ratio ≈ 95% → origin egress ≈ 5% × 26 Tbps ≈ ~1.3 Tbps # the CDN absorbs 95% of the 26 Tbps
Takeaway

~375 TB fits comfortably across a distributed CDN fleet, so a high hit ratio is achievable and origin traffic collapses to a small fraction of total egress. This one number justifies the whole edge-caching strategy.

3. High-Level Architecture

Trace one video's life. On the write path an upload lands in a raw blob store, a transcode pipeline fans it into the rendition ladder and writes packaged segments back to blob storage, and metadata is recorded. On the read path a viewer's player pulls segments from the CDN (which pulls from the rendition store on a miss), guided by the feed/recommender and the metadata service.

Write path (top, left→right): uploader → upload service → raw store → transcode → packaged renditions → CDN. Read path: CDN → viewer's player, steered by the feed/recommender; the metadata DB records catalog data the upload service writes and the feed reads.

4. Upload & Storage

Uploads are large (hundreds of MB to many GB) and often come from flaky mobile networks. Two ideas make this robust and keep the app servers out of the data path.

Resumable, chunked upload via presigned URLs

  • The client asks the Upload Service to initiate an upload; the service returns a set of presigned URLs (short-lived, signed links that authorize a direct PUT to the blob store) and an upload ID.
  • The client splits the file into chunks (e.g. 5–10 MB) and uploads them directly to object storage, in parallel. The app tier never proxies the bytes — it only issues URLs and coordinates.
  • If the connection drops, the client resumes by re-uploading only the missing chunks (each chunk is idempotent by its part number). A final "complete" call assembles the object and the service enqueues a transcode job.
Why presigned + direct-to-storage

It removes the upload bytes from your compute tier entirely — no server holds a multi-GB stream in memory — and lets the object store's own scale/durability do the heavy lifting. This is the standard pattern for large-file ingest.

Object storage & content-hash dedup

Store both raw and packaged files in object/blob storage (cheap, durable, effectively unbounded, HTTP-addressable) rather than a database — video bytes never belong in a relational row. Compute a content hash (e.g. SHA-256) of the upload; if the hash already exists, you can dedup — skip re-storing and re-transcoding an identical file (reposts, re-uploads). Dedup saves both storage and the (expensive) transcode compute.

Dedup is exact-match only

A content hash catches byte-identical re-uploads. A re-encode, a trim, or a watermark changes the bytes and defeats it — near-duplicate detection is a separate, ML-style problem (perceptual hashing / fingerprinting) and is out of scope for the ingest fast path. Say that so you don't overclaim.

5. Transcode Pipeline — a Core Challenge

This is where a single raw upload becomes many streamable renditions. It is a classic queue + autoscaling worker pool batch system, and the interview reward is in getting its reliability properties right.

One raw upload fans out to every rung of the bitrate ladder (five parallel encode jobs), then the packager segments each rendition into ~6 s chunks with manifests and writes them to the rendition store for the CDN.

How the pipeline works

  1. Enqueue: on upload-complete, push a transcode job onto a durable queue. The queue decouples bursty uploads from finite compute and absorbs spikes (backpressure).
  2. Fan-out: a worker (or a coordinator) creates one encode task per rendition — the ladder rungs are independent and run in parallel across the fleet.
  3. Segment: long videos are split into chunks so each chunk can be encoded on a different worker and reassembled — this parallelizes a single big video and bounds per-task time.
  4. Package: encoded output is cut into ~6 s streaming segments and paired with manifests (playlists) per rendition (§6), then written to the rendition store.
  5. Publish: mark the video READY in metadata once all renditions land; only then is it playable.

Reliability properties that win points

  • Idempotent + retriable: a transcode job is a pure function of (raw file, rendition spec). If a worker dies mid-job, retry it — re-encoding the same input yields the same output, so retries are safe. Key outputs by a deterministic name so a re-run overwrites cleanly rather than duplicating.
  • At-least-once queue + dedup: queues typically deliver at-least-once, so a job may run twice; idempotent outputs make that harmless. Track per-rendition completion so you never mark READY prematurely.
  • Priority lanes: short clips and creator/live content jump ahead of long back-catalog re-encodes — a short video should be watchable in seconds, so give it a high-priority queue.
  • Progressive publish: publish low resolutions first (they encode fastest) so the video becomes watchable before 1080p finishes.
Interview move

Say "transcode is embarrassingly parallel and idempotent, so I retry infrastructure failures freely and autoscale the worker fleet on queue depth." That single sentence signals you understand both the compute shape and the failure model.

6. Delivery: CDN + Adaptive Bitrate

Delivery is the read path — the 26 Tbps side of the system. Two ideas carry it: segmented adaptive-bitrate streaming (HLS/DASH) and edge caching on a CDN.

HLS / DASH: a manifest + segmented chunks

Instead of one giant file per rendition, each rendition is a series of short (~6 s) segments, described by a manifest (a playlist). There is a master manifest listing all renditions, and a variant manifest per rendition listing its segments in order. HLS (HTTP Live Streaming) and DASH (Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP) are the two standard formats; both are just HTTP requests for small files, so any CDN and any firewall handles them.

The master manifest lists variant tracks; each track is a row of ~6 s segments. The red path shows the player switching rendition segment-by-segment as measured bandwidth rises and falls — that is adaptive bitrate (ABR).

Adaptive bitrate (ABR): the player decides

The player downloads the master manifest, measures how fast segments arrive (and how full its buffer is), and chooses, for each next segment, the highest rendition its current bandwidth can sustain. If the network degrades, it steps down (720p → 480p) to avoid a stall; if it improves, it steps up. Because segment boundaries align across renditions, the player can switch cleanly at each ~6 s boundary. The intelligence lives in the client — the server just hosts static segments.

Why segments instead of whole files

  • Adaptivity: switching quality mid-stream only works if you can pick a different rendition for the next small chunk — impossible with one monolithic file.
  • Fast start & seek: the player fetches only the first segment to begin playback (low time-to-first-frame) and jumps to any segment on a seek, instead of downloading the whole video.
  • Cacheability: small immutable segments are ideal CDN cache objects — each is fetched by many viewers and cached independently; a partial watch still warms the cache for the popular early segments.
  • Resilience: a dropped segment is a tiny, cheap retry, not a restart of a huge download.

Edge caching on the CDN

Segments are served from CDN edge nodes close to the viewer. On a cache miss the edge pulls from the rendition store (origin) once, then serves the hot copy to everyone nearby. With the ~375 TB hot set (§2) fitting the fleet, hit ratios of ~95% are realistic, so origin egress collapses to a small fraction (~1.3 Tbps) of the 26 Tbps total. Segments are immutable (a segment's bytes never change), so they can be cached with long TTLs and never revalidated — a big reason video caches so well.

7. Metadata & Data Model

The bytes live in blob storage; the facts about a video live in databases. Separate them: never store video in a row, never store the catalog in the object store.

Core entities

EntityKey fieldsStore & why
Video metadatavideo_id, owner_id, title, description, tags, duration, status, created_at, rendition URIsRelatively low write rate, needs search/filter; fits a relational or document DB, sharded by video_id.
View countvideo_id → countExtremely high write rate; kept approximate/async (below), often in a counter store.
Commentscomment_id, video_id, user_id, text, parent_id, created_atHigh volume, append-heavy, partitioned by video_id; a wide-column/NoSQL store fits.
Likes / reactions(user_id, video_id) → reactionHigh volume; counts aggregated async like views.

SQL vs. NoSQL — pick per workload

  • Video/catalog metadata: modest write volume, benefits from secondary indexes and search — a relational or document DB is fine, and a dedicated search index handles title/tag queries.
  • Comments, likes, view events: massive append-only volume with a natural partition key (video_id) and no need for cross-entity transactions — a horizontally scalable NoSQL/wide-column store fits better.

Sharding: shard by video_id so all data for one video (metadata, comments, counts) co-locates and a hot video's traffic is bounded to its shards; add read replicas and a cache in front for the read-heavy watch page.

View counts at scale — approximate & async

Never synchronously increment a shared counter per view

At 3 billion views/day (§2), a synchronous UPDATE ... SET views = views + 1 on a hot row for a viral video is a write-contention meltdown. Instead: emit a view event to a stream, aggregate counts asynchronously (batch or streaming), and update the displayed count periodically. The number a viewer sees is eventually consistent and slightly stale — which is completely acceptable for a view count, and the same async, approximate treatment applies to like counts.

8. Feed / Recommendation & the Engagement Loop

The feed decides what to watch next — the home feed of a large video-sharing platform, or the endless For-You feed of a short-video app. Keep the ML generic; the interview cares about the system around it.

Two-stage funnel: candidate generation recalls a large set cheaply, ranking scores them precisely, the top-N become the feed. Watch signals flow back and continuously retrain/re-rank — the engagement loop.

Two-stage funnel

  • Candidate generation (recall): from the whole catalog, cheaply pull a few thousand plausible videos — from follows/subscriptions, "similar to what you watched" (embedding nearest-neighbors), and trending. Optimizes for recall, not precision.
  • Ranking: a heavier model scores those candidates for this user/context and orders them; the top-N become the feed. This is where most of the ML compute goes, but only on a small candidate set.

The engagement loop

Watch signals — watch time, finish rate, likes, skips, re-watches — flow back as features and training labels, so the ranker adapts to what actually holds attention. For a short-video app this loop is tight and dominant: the feed reacts within a few videos.

Push vs. pull for the feed

  • Pull (compute on request): build the feed when the user opens the app. Always fresh, no wasted precompute for inactive users — but adds latency to the request and recomputes on every refresh. Best for the highly personalized, real-time For-You feed.
  • Push (precompute): compute feeds ahead of time and store them, so a request is a cheap read. Great for stable home feeds, wasteful if content/personalization changes fast.
  • Most systems are hybrid: precompute a candidate pool, rank on request. (This mirrors the fan-out push/pull trade-off from the Social Feed chapter — same idea, different signals.)

Cold start

A brand-new user (no history) or a brand-new video (no engagement) can't be ranked from personal signals. Bootstrap with popularity/trending and lightweight onboarding signals for new users, and give new videos an exploration boost (show them to a small sample to gather signal) before committing.

Privacy & consent — say it briefly

The engagement loop runs on behavioral data, so treat it responsibly: honor user consent and regional privacy rules, let users clear watch history and opt out of personalization, and avoid using sensitive attributes as ranking signals. A senior candidate names the privacy/consent dimension without turning the answer into a policy essay.

9. Scaling & Caching

CDN hit ratio on the hot set

The whole read path leans on the power law: a small hot set serves most views (§2). Keep hot segments at the edge with long TTLs (segments are immutable), and the ~26 Tbps of viewer egress is absorbed almost entirely by the CDN, leaving origin to serve the long tail (~1.3 Tbps at a 95% hit ratio). Use tiered caching (edge → regional → origin) so a cold segment is fetched from origin once per region, not once per edge.

View-count aggregation

As in §7: view/like events go to a stream, are aggregated asynchronously (streaming or micro-batch), and the displayed count is updated periodically. This turns billions of hot-row writes into cheap append-only events plus a background rollup, and shields the metadata DB from viral write storms.

Thumbnail service

Thumbnails are tiny images requested even more often than video (every feed/search result shows one). Generate a few sizes during transcode, store in blob storage, and serve through the CDN like any static asset — a separate, simple, extremely cacheable path. Don't let thumbnail traffic touch the video pipeline.

Multi-region

  • Storage: replicate the rendition store across regions (and let the CDN pull from the nearest) for durability and locality.
  • Metadata: replicate read-heavy metadata to each region with read replicas; keep writes to a primary (or use a globally distributed DB) since the catalog write rate is low.
  • Transcode: the batch fleet can run wherever compute is cheap/available — it's asynchronous and not latency-sensitive, unlike delivery.

10. Trade-offs & Interview Framing

Drive the conversation: clarify scope → estimate (§2) → draw the write/read spine (§3) → go deep on the two core challenges (transcode §5, delivery §6) → cover metadata/feed and cost. Then handle the probes below.

Q: Why transcode one upload into many renditions instead of storing the original and serving it?
Viewers have wildly different screens and bandwidth. A single file forces everyone onto one quality — stalls for slow connections, wasted bytes for small screens, and no adaptive switching. The ladder lets each viewer stream the best quality their network sustains and switch mid-stream (ABR). It costs ~375 MB of durable storage per 5-min video and a big batch-compute fleet (§2), but that write-once cost is amortized over ~300 reads each (§2), so optimizing the read/delivery experience wins overwhelmingly.

Q: HLS/DASH vs. progressive download — why segmented streaming?
Progressive download fetches one whole file: no mid-stream quality adaptation, slow start (you wait to buffer a big file), expensive seeks, and poor cacheability. Segmented ABR fetches small immutable ~6 s chunks described by a manifest, so the player starts fast, seeks cheaply, adapts quality per segment, and the CDN caches each chunk independently. The only cost is more objects/manifests to manage — worth it at scale.

Q: Push vs. pull for the feed?
Pull (compute on request) keeps the feed fresh and personalized, ideal for a real-time For-You feed, at the cost of per-request latency. Push (precompute) makes reads cheap for stable home feeds but wastes work when personalization changes fast. Most go hybrid: precompute a candidate pool, rank on request. Match the choice to how fast the feed must react to fresh signals.

Q: How do you count views at 3 billion/day?
Never synchronously increment a shared row. Emit a view event to a stream, aggregate asynchronously (streaming/batch), and update the displayed count periodically. The count is eventually consistent and slightly stale — acceptable for views — and the hot-row write storm disappears. Add dedup/bot-filtering in the aggregation stage so a reload or a bot doesn't inflate counts.

Q: How do you control storage cost as the catalog grows ~1.4 EB/year?
Delete or cold-archive the raw upload after successful transcode; tier renditions by popularity (hot on fast storage/CDN, cold tail on cheap archival storage); apply expiry/retention to abandoned or policy-violating content; drop unnecessary rungs for videos that are never watched in high resolution; and consider re-encoding the cold tail with more efficient codecs. Dedup identical re-uploads (§4). Storage tiering + expiry is the answer interviewers listen for.

Q: How consistent are like/view counts, and is that OK?
They are eventually consistent — updated asynchronously, so a viewer may see a slightly stale number. That's the right trade: exactness on a view count buys nothing and would cost enormous write contention. Contrast with something that must be strongly consistent (a payment, from the Payment System chapter) — video counts are explicitly the opposite end of that spectrum, and saying so shows you match consistency to the stakes.

One-line summary

A video platform is a write-once/read-millions system: ingest bytes straight to blob storage, fan each upload into a rendition ladder with an idempotent autoscaling transcode fleet, package into small immutable segments, and serve them with adaptive bitrate over a CDN whose edge cache absorbs the tens-of-Tbps egress — while metadata, view counts, and the recommendation feed run on separate, mostly-async, eventually-consistent paths.
