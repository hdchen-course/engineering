Design a Device Lab / Test-Farm Platform

The software platform that drives racks of real, physical devices — phones, IoT gadgets, embedded boards — so they can run automated tests around the clock. This is the orchestration layer, not the firmware or RF: an inventory, a scheduler, and a fleet of hosts wired to hardware. A valuable niche anywhere hardware is built at scale, Taiwan very much included.
Read this like a story

Start at the top even if you already run a test rig at your desk — the sections build from "why does a company wire hundreds of phones into a rack" up to "keep a dead board from wedging the whole queue across three sites." The two diagrams (the control-plane/data-plane split and the device state machine) are the spine; everything else hangs off them.

Scope — the software layer, not the RF

This chapter is about the software that schedules, provisions, and observes physical devices for automated testing. It is not about writing firmware, radio/PHY stacks, or bring-up of a single board on your bench. Where it touches hardware — flashing an image, power-cycling a stuck board — we treat that as an operation the platform commands, not as electronics you design. If you have read Test Automation at Scale and Observability & Dashboards, this is the hardware-facing cousin of both.

1. The Problem & Who Does This

A phone maker ships an app and an OS that must work on 40 device models across 6 Android versions. A smart-speaker team must prove every firmware build still boots, connects to Wi-Fi, and responds to a wake word. A car-infotainment supplier must run a 4-hour soak test on the actual board that ships in the vehicle. None of this can be faked in a pure emulator — the bugs live in real hardware: a specific SoC, a flaky USB hub, a sensor, a modem. So companies build device labs: racks of real devices under test (DUTs) wired to host machines, running automated tests continuously.

The device-lab / test-farm platform is the software that makes that rack usable by hundreds of engineers and thousands of CI jobs at once. Without it you get a graveyard of phones taped to a desk, "borrowed" by whoever shouts loudest, half of them bricked and nobody sure which. With it, a developer (or a CI pipeline) says "run this test suite on any Pixel-class device on Android 14" and the platform finds a healthy one, flashes the build, runs the test, streams the logs back, and releases the device — all without a human touching the rack.

Who builds and runs these — the real roles

These are established titles, not invented ones. If you are targeting one, the vocabulary below is what shows up in the job description:

  • Test / Device Lab Infrastructure Engineer — owns the platform: inventory, scheduler, host agents, dashboards.
  • Test Automation Infrastructure Engineer / SDET (infrastructure) — builds the systems that run tests at scale rather than writing individual test cases.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) Software Engineer — connects real hardware into an automated test loop (common in automotive, robotics, aerospace, consumer electronics).
  • Device Orchestration / Test Platform Engineer — the scheduling-and-fleet side specifically.
  • Lab / Bench Automation Engineer — power control, instrument control, physical layer of the rack.

Why this niche is valuable — especially in a hardware hub

Every company that ships physical products at volume needs one of these. Taiwan is a global hub for phones, laptops, networking gear, IoT modules, and board-level manufacturing (ODM/OEM work), so the demand for engineers who can automate validation across many devices is concrete and local. The skill is transferable: the same platform shape serves a phone OEM, a networking-equipment vendor, a wearables startup, or an automotive supplier. It sits at the seam between software engineering and manufacturing/QA — a seam that is hard to offshore because the devices are physically here.

2. Requirements

Pin these down out loud before drawing boxes. The functional list is "what a user can ask the platform to do"; the non-functional list is "what makes it trustworthy at scale."

Functional requirements

  • Register & inventory devices. Enrol a device with its capabilities (model, OS/firmware version, screen, sensors, connectivity) and track where it is physically attached.
  • Provision / flash. Put a known build/image onto the device and bring it to a clean, known state before a test.
  • Schedule jobs onto suitable devices. Match a test job's requirements ("Android 14, has NFC") to an available healthy device, honoring priority and team quotas.
  • Run tests & collect everything. Execute the suite, stream logs, capture screenshots/video, gather performance and device telemetry, and store artifacts.
  • Report results. Per-job pass/fail with links to artifacts, plus fleet-level dashboards and trends.
  • Interactive access. Let a human grab a specific device to reproduce a bug (remote screen / shell), the way STF and Device Farm both offer.

Non-functional requirements

Must be true

  • High utilization. Devices are the expensive, finite resource — idle hardware is wasted capital. Target high busy-time.
  • Isolation between jobs. One job must never leak state (files, accounts, cached Wi-Fi creds) into the next on the same device.
  • Flaky/dead-device detection. Automatically spot devices that fail everything or stop responding, and quarantine them.
  • Scale to thousands of devices across many hosts and potentially multiple physical sites.
  • Observability. Know at a glance what every device is doing and why a job failed.

Explicitly out of scope (say so)

  • Writing the tests themselves (that's the product teams).
  • Firmware / RF / PHY development.
  • Being a load-testing tool for backends — a device lab tests the device, not your servers (Firebase Test Lab makes the same disclaimer).
  • Emulation as the primary path — emulators complement but do not replace real hardware here.
The one-liner that frames everything

"The device is the scarce, unreliable resource." Every design decision below — the scheduler, the state machine, the quarantine policy, the timeouts — exists because physical hardware is finite and flaky in ways stateless cloud VMs are not.

3. High-Level Architecture

The clean way to reason about a device lab is the classic split every large fleet system uses: a control plane that decides and remembers, and a data plane that physically touches the devices. The control plane is ordinary stateless-ish software you scale like any web backend; the data plane is the hard, physical part.

Control plane (blue/dashed, left) decides and remembers; data plane (teal/dashed, right) physically drives devices. Solid arrows are the request/result path; dashed arrows are reads. The scheduler dispatches to a host agent, which flashes and runs the DUT, heartbeats device state back to the registry, writes results, and uploads artifacts the dashboard links to.

The pieces, in one line each

  • API / Gateway — authenticates callers and accepts job submissions; the single front door.
  • Job Queue — holds pending jobs ordered by priority; decouples submission rate from device availability.
  • Scheduler — the brain: pulls a job, reads the registry, picks a healthy capable free device, and dispatches (§5).
  • Device Registry / Inventory — the source of truth for every device: capabilities, current state, which host it's on (§4).
  • Device Host + Agent — the machine physically wired to devices; the agent flashes, runs, streams, and recovers them (§6).
  • Result Store — structured pass/fail and metadata per job, queried by dashboards.
  • Artifact Store — bulky blobs (logs, screenshots, video, crash dumps) in object storage, referenced by URL.
  • Dashboard & Reporting — fleet health and result trends for humans (§7).
Why the plane split matters

The control plane can be redundant, load-balanced, and restarted freely — it holds no irreplaceable state beyond its databases. The data plane is pinned to physical reality: a given device is reachable from exactly one host, and if that host dies, those devices are unreachable until it recovers. Keeping the two mentally separate is what stops candidates from brushing aside a fundamentally physical problem with a vague "just add more replicas."

4. Device Model & Inventory

The registry is the heart of the control plane. Everything the scheduler does is a query against it, so its data model and its state machine deserve real thought.

Capabilities & attributes

A device is described by what a job can select on. Split them into stable attributes (rarely change) and dynamic state (changes constantly):

The device state machine

A physical device is never simply "up or down." It moves through a small set of states, and the platform's correctness depends on every transition being explicit. This is the second spine diagram of the chapter.

Device state machine. Solid arrows are normal/forced transitions; dashed arrows are recoveries. A device becomes schedulable only in Available; the scheduler moves it to Reserved, the agent to Running, and on a clean finish it resets back to Available. Failed health checks go to Unhealthy; repeated failures escalate to Quarantined. Lost heartbeats mark it Offline regardless of prior state.
  • Available — healthy and idle; the only state the scheduler will pick from.
  • Reserved — the scheduler has claimed it for a specific job (a short-lived lock so two jobs can't grab the same device — the analog of the atomic check in a rate limiter).
  • Running — the agent is flashing or executing on it.
  • Unhealthy — a health check failed once; taken out of rotation pending recovery.
  • Quarantined — it keeps failing; removed from scheduling and flagged for a human, so it stops poisoning jobs (§7).
  • Offline — no heartbeat (host crashed, USB dropped, power lost); unreachable until it reappears.

Health checks & heartbeats

Two mechanisms keep the registry honest:

  • Heartbeats. Each host agent periodically reports "these devices are attached and in state X." If a heartbeat is missing for longer than a threshold, the control plane marks those devices offline and requeues any job that was on them. This is what prevents a crashed host from silently holding devices hostage.
  • Health checks. Between jobs (and on a schedule), the agent runs a cheap probe: is the device reachable, does it boot, is battery/temperature sane, does a trivial command return? A failed probe pushes the device to unhealthy; a passed one after recovery returns it to available.
Interview signal

Distinguishing heartbeat (liveness of the host→device link) from health check (fitness of the device to run a test) is a senior distinction. A device can be heartbeating fine yet be unfit (overheating, storage full); it can also be perfectly healthy yet offline because its host crashed.

5. Scheduling

The scheduler is where a device lab earns or wastes its money. Its job: take a pending test job and bind it to the best currently-available device, quickly and fairly, without letting one big job starve everyone else.

Capability matching with label selectors

A job doesn't ask for a specific device — it asks for a kind of device, exactly like a Kubernetes pod's nodeSelector (see the Kubernetes chapter). The scheduler filters the registry to devices that satisfy the selector and are available:

# a job's device requirement
require: model in [pixel-8, pixel-8a]
require: os == android-14
require: capabilities ⊇ {nfc}
prefer: labels.pool == ci # soft preference, not a hard filter

candidates = registry.filter(state == available AND matches(require))
if candidates is empty: keep job queued (or reject if no such device exists at all)
else: pick by policy → reserve → dispatch
Impossible vs. temporarily-unavailable

Separate "no device in the whole fleet can ever satisfy this" (fail fast with a clear error) from "all matching devices are busy right now" (keep queued and wait). Conflating them either wedges a job forever or rejects a satisfiable request. This mirrors a pod stuck Pending because no node matches vs. because nodes are full.

Fairness & quotas across teams

On a shared lab, one team's 5,000-job nightly run must not lock out another team's single urgent job. Standard levers:

  • Quotas / pools. Reserve slices of the fleet per team (via labels), or cap concurrent devices per team. Guarantees a minimum even under contention.
  • Fair queuing. Round-robin across teams' queues rather than strict FIFO, so a burst from one submitter doesn't monopolize devices — the same idea as fair scheduling in a rate limiter.
  • Priority. Presubmit/CI-blocking jobs outrank best-effort soak tests. Use priority to order, but keep a floor so low-priority work isn't starved forever.

Bin-packing, batching & head-of-line blocking

  • Bin-packing for utilization. When a job needs many devices, prefer packing them onto devices/hosts that maximize overall busy-time, but balance against isolation (don't cram so tightly that one flaky host takes out a whole run).
  • Batching. A suite that must run across 30 models is one logical job fanned out to 30 device reservations; track it as a parent with children so partial failures are visible.
  • Avoid head-of-line blocking. A job at the front of the queue that needs a rare device (only two exist, both busy) must not block ready-to-run jobs behind it that want common devices. Let the scheduler look past the head and dispatch any job whose device is free — a queue you can scan, not a strict FIFO.
This is a matching problem, not just a queue

Because jobs are heterogeneous (different device requirements) and devices are heterogeneous (different capabilities), scheduling is a bipartite matching between pending jobs and free devices, re-evaluated whenever either side changes (a job arrives, a device frees up). Saying that out loud — and then choosing a pragmatic greedy policy (priority-ordered, skip-the-blocked) rather than a perfect but slow optimum — is the senior answer.

6. The Device Host / Agent

The agent is a small daemon on the machine physically wired to a set of devices (over USB, serial, or network). It is the data plane's hands: the control plane only ever asks; the agent does. Everything hardware-specific and failure-prone lives here.

The job lifecycle on a host

# agent receives a dispatched job for one attached device
1. lock the device (confirm the reservation; refuse if not Reserved)
2. provision / flash the requested build/image → known clean state
3. health-check post-flash (boots? reachable?) — abort early if not
4. run the test suite; stream logs & telemetry live to the control plane
5. collect artifacts (screenshots, video, crash dumps) → artifact store
6. reset the device (wipe state, clear accounts/caches) for isolation
7. release → report result → device back to Available (or Unhealthy)

Provision, flash & reset — the physical primitives

These are exactly the primitives the open-source tools expose: Labgrid, for instance, provides power/reset control, serial-console interaction, and binary upload/bootstrap over USB; LAVA structures every job as deploy → boot → test. Concretely the agent can:

  • Flash an OS/firmware image (fastboot, a bootloader over serial, an OTA package).
  • Reset to a clean state between jobs — factory-wipe a phone, re-flash a board — so job N+1 never inherits job N's leftovers.
  • Recover a stuck device by escalating: soft reboot → force reboot → power-cycle via a controllable power switch/USB hub. The ability to physically cut power to one device is what turns a "bricked, needs a human" into "recovered in 30 seconds."

Isolation

Two dimensions of isolation matter:

  • On the device: wipe user data and caches between jobs; the reset step (6 above) is the guarantee. Skipping it is the classic source of "passes alone, fails in the farm" bugs.
  • On the host: if one host drives many devices, run each job's harness in its own sandbox (container/process) so a runaway test can't corrupt a sibling job or the agent itself. Bound each job's CPU/RAM/USB access.
The agent must be paranoid

The agent talks to hardware that lies, hangs, and disconnects mid-command. Every operation needs a timeout and a defined failure action. An agent that blocks forever on a wedged flash call takes its device — and sometimes its whole host — out of service. "What does the agent do when flash hangs?" is a common probe: answer with timeout → escalate to power-cycle → if still dead, mark unhealthy and move on.

7. Results, Telemetry & Flakiness

A test that ran but whose result you can't trust — or can't inspect — is worthless. This section is about turning raw runs into signal, and about the defining pain of hardware labs: flakiness.

What to collect, and where it goes

  • Structured result (pass/fail per test, timings, exit codes) → the result store (a queryable database). Small, indexed, drives dashboards.
  • Bulky artifacts (full logs, screenshots, video, perf traces, crash dumps) → the artifact store (object storage), referenced by URL. Never stuff megabytes of logs into the result database.
  • Device telemetry (battery, temperature, CPU, memory, connectivity) sampled during the run — both to diagnose failures and to feed the health score.
Split small-and-structured from big-and-blobby

This two-store split (queryable metadata vs. object-storage blobs) is the same pattern used across the book — it keeps the hot query path fast while artifacts scale cheaply. See Observability & Dashboards for the metrics/logs/traces framing.

Flaky tests vs. flaky devices — the crucial split

A flaky test is non-deterministic logic: the same test passes and fails on the same healthy device depending on timing, order, or a race. A flaky (or dead) device is bad hardware: it fails or degrades many different tests that pass fine elsewhere. Telling them apart is the single most important analysis a device lab does, because the fix is opposite:

SignalFlaky TESTFlaky DEVICE
Fails across many devices?Yes — same test, many devicesNo — one device, many tests
Same test passes on a rerun on healthy HW?Often yesTest is fine elsewhere
Correlated to one device_id?NoStrongly
FixQuarantine/fix the test; the hardware is fineQuarantine the device; the test is fine
# the discriminating question
if one test fails on many different healthy devices → flaky TEST
if one device fails many different tests that pass elsewhere → flaky/dead DEVICE

Quarantine policy

Maintain a rolling health score per device (e.g. recent pass-rate on a canary/health suite, plus telemetry sanity). When it crosses a threshold — or the device fails a fixed number of consecutive health checks — automatically move it unhealthy → quarantined and page/notify. Symmetrically, a quarantined device that a human repairs (or that recovers and passes a probation run) returns to the pool. Quarantine fast: one bad device left schedulable can silently fail dozens of jobs and get innocent code changes blamed.

The blame-the-code trap

The most damaging failure mode of a device lab is a flaky device that stays in rotation: it fails jobs semi-randomly, developers waste hours debugging code that was never broken, and trust in the whole platform collapses. Aggressive automatic quarantine — biased toward pulling a suspect device out rather than risking bad signal — is the antidote. It's better to lose one device's capacity than to emit false failures.

Dashboards

Two audiences: fleet health (how many devices available/running/quarantined/offline, utilization, per-model capacity) for the lab operators, and result trends (pass-rate over time, flaky-test leaderboard, slowest suites) for the product teams. A device stuck quarantined for days, or utilization collapsing, should be visible at a glance.

8. Reliability & Scale

The recurring theme: a device lab must degrade gracefully around unreliable hardware, and grow from one rack to many sites without a rewrite.

A dead device (or host) must not wedge the queue

This is a reliability question interviewers often care about, because it's specific to physical fleets. Defenses, layered:

  • Reservations are leases, not locks. A device is Reserved/Running with a lease/timeout. If the job doesn't complete or the host stops heartbeating, the lease expires, the device is reclaimed, and the job is requeued — no device is held forever by a crashed job.
  • Per-step timeouts. Flash, boot, and test each have their own timeout; exceeding one triggers recovery (power-cycle) then failure, rather than an indefinite hang.
  • Auto-recovery before giving up. Escalate soft reboot → force reboot → power-cycle; only after that fails does the device go unhealthy/quarantined and out of rotation.
  • Requeue idempotently. A requeued job must be safe to re-run (fresh flash + reset guarantees a clean device), so a mid-run host crash costs a retry, not a corrupt result.
"I'd make every device reservation a lease with a timeout and give flash/boot/test their own step timeouts. If a host stops heartbeating, its leases expire, the devices are marked offline, and their jobs requeue automatically — so one wedged device or dead host costs a retry, never a stuck queue. Recovery escalates to a physical power-cycle before I quarantine the device."

Horizontal scale of hosts

Devices scale by adding hosts, and hosts are embarrassingly parallel: each drives its own devices independently, heartbeating to a shared control plane. The control plane (API, scheduler, registry, stores) scales like any stateless-ish backend — replicate it, shard the registry/result store by device or team if needed. The scheduler is the one component to watch: keep its match loop fast (index the registry by capability) so it isn't the bottleneck as the fleet grows to thousands.

Multi-site labs

Real fleets span buildings, cities, or countries (a Taiwan lab plus one overseas). Two workable shapes:

Regional control planes (federated)
  • Each site runs its own scheduler + registry; a thin global layer routes a job to a site that has the device.
  • Survives a cross-site network partition — each site keeps working locally.
  • Latency to hardware stays local (flashing gigabytes over a WAN is painful).
One global control plane
  • Single scheduler over all sites — simplest mental model, one queue.
  • But a WAN partition can strand a whole site, and every heartbeat/flash command crosses the WAN.
  • Fine at two nearby sites; strains as sites and distance grow.

The usual answer is federated: keep the data plane (hosts, heartbeats, flashing, artifacts) local to each site for latency and partition-tolerance, and put a light global scheduler/inventory on top for cross-site routing and a single unified view.

9. Trade-offs & Lessons

Real vs. emulated / simulated devices

Emulators / simulators
  • Cheap, infinitely scalable, fast to spin up, no physical maintenance.
  • Great for early/functional tests and huge fan-out (Firebase Test Lab offers virtual devices for exactly this).
Real devices
  • The only way to catch SoC/driver/sensor/modem/thermal/battery bugs — the ones that ship.
  • Expensive, finite, flaky, need racks, power, and physical maintenance.

The mature answer is a pyramid: run the bulk of tests on emulators, and reserve the scarce real devices for the tests that genuinely need hardware (hardware-specific integration, performance, certification). Don't burn a physical device on a test an emulator would catch.

Buy vs. build

Managed clouds (Firebase Test Lab, AWS Device Farm) let you skip owning racks entirely — pay per device-minute. That's ideal when your matrix is mainstream phones and you don't want to run physical infrastructure. You build your own lab when you need devices no cloud stocks (pre-release hardware, your own boards, custom peripherals, specific network/RF setups), when volume makes per-minute pricing painful, or when devices can't leave the building for security/compliance. Many hardware companies do both: cloud for app-on-mainstream-phone coverage, in-house lab for their actual product hardware.

The flaky-hardware reality

The lesson every device-lab engineer learns: hardware fails constantly and unpredictably. USB ports drop, cables wear out, batteries swell, devices overheat, a firmware bug bricks a unit. The platform's entire reliability design — heartbeats, health checks, leases, timeouts, power-cycle recovery, aggressive quarantine — exists to make an individual device's flakiness invisible to the fleet's users. You are not trying to make hardware reliable; you are building a system that stays trustworthy despite unreliable hardware.

Utilization vs. isolation

The core operational tension. Pack devices tightly and reset lightly, and utilization soars — but state leaks between jobs and results get flaky. Reset thoroughly and sandbox everything, and results are trustworthy — but every job pays a re-flash/wipe tax that lowers utilization. There's no universal right point: a billing-critical or certification suite leans hard toward isolation; a cheap smoke test can afford lighter resets. Making that trade-off explicit per job class is the sign of someone who has actually run a lab.

10. Interview Framing

How to drive the conversation, then the probes an interviewer might throw.

  1. Clarify (1–2 min): What devices (phones / boards / IoT)? Who submits (devs, CI, both)? Real hardware, emulators, or both? One site or many? Scale — dozens or thousands?
  2. Split control plane vs. data plane (§3) — this framing alone signals seniority.
  3. Nail the device state machine & registry (§4) as the source of truth.
  4. Scheduler as capability matching + fairness (§5), then reliability via leases/timeouts (§8).
  5. Close on flakiness (§7) — the thing that makes device labs uniquely hard.

What interviewers probe (with one-liners)

Q: How do you schedule a job onto heterogeneous devices?
Treat it as bipartite matching: filter the registry to devices that are available and satisfy the job's capability/label selector, then pick by policy (priority, quotas, bin-packing), reserve the device with a short lease, and dispatch. Separate "no device can ever match" (fail fast) from "all matches are busy" (stay queued).

Q: How do you tell a flaky device from a flaky test?
Correlate failures. A test failing across many healthy devices is a flaky test (fix/quarantine the test). A single device failing many different tests that pass elsewhere is a flaky device (quarantine the device via its health score). The discriminator is whether failures cluster by test or by device_id.

Q: How do you keep a dead device from blocking the queue?
Reservations are leases with timeouts, and flash/boot/test each have step timeouts. If a host stops heartbeating or a step hangs, the lease expires, the device is marked offline/unhealthy, and the job requeues idempotently. Recovery escalates to a physical power-cycle before the device is quarantined — so one bad device costs a retry, never a stuck queue.

Q: Real devices or emulators — how do you choose?
A pyramid: emulators for the bulk (cheap, scalable, catch functional bugs), scarce real devices reserved for tests that need actual hardware (SoC/driver/sensor/thermal/performance/certification). Buy managed cloud (Firebase Test Lab, AWS Device Farm) for mainstream phones; build in-house for pre-release or custom hardware that no cloud stocks.

Q: How do you scale to N sites?
Federate: keep the data plane (hosts, heartbeats, flashing, artifacts) local to each site for latency and partition-tolerance, with a light global scheduler/inventory on top for cross-site routing and one unified view. Avoid a single global control plane whose every heartbeat and multi-gigabyte flash crosses the WAN and whose partition strands a whole site.

Q: What's the hardest part in practice?
Hardware is flaky and finite. The whole reliability design — heartbeats, health checks, leases, timeouts, power-cycle recovery, aggressive quarantine, thorough inter-job reset — exists to make one device's unreliability invisible to the fleet's users, and to never emit a false failure that gets innocent code blamed.

One-line summary

A device lab is a control plane (API, queue, scheduler, registry, result store) driving a data plane of host agents wired to real devices. Model each device as an explicit state machine, schedule by capability + fairness with leased reservations, isolate and reset between jobs, and — above all — detect and quarantine flaky hardware fast so the fleet stays trustworthy despite devices that constantly fail.
