Device Provisioning & Identity

How a device earns a trusted identity and secret keys at the factory — before it is ever allowed to talk to the cloud
📚 Google Security Systems — Overview  ›  Part VII · Chapter 9 — Device Provisioning & Identity

What "provisioning" means

Analogy first. Think of a brand-new smart doorbell rolling off a factory line. Right now it is a blank stranger — nothing about it proves it is a real, genuine device rather than a fake someone built in a garage. Provisioning is the moment we give that device its passport and its private signature: a unique identity, plus secret keys that only it holds. From then on it can prove "I am genuinely device #A7F3, made by this company," and nobody can convincingly pretend to be it.

Tiny concrete example. When your doorbell later connects to the cloud, the cloud asks: "Prove who you are." The doorbell signs a random challenge with its secret key and presents its certificate. The cloud checks the signature and the certificate — like a border officer checking a passport and a live signature — and only then lets it in. All of that is possible only because provisioning happened first.

The one-sentence model: provisioning is the birth certificate step — each device gets a unique key pair and a certificate signed by a trusted authority, recorded centrally, so that later anyone can verify the device is genuine without having to trust the factory or phone home to a secret master key.

Why trust must be established before the device talks to the cloud

The cloud has a circular problem: it should only accept data from real devices, but how does it recognize a real device the very first time it connects? If the answer were "anyone who connects is trusted," an attacker could spin up a million fake doorbells and flood, spoof, or poison the system. So the trust has to be built in earlier — at manufacture — when we can control the environment. The device shows up to the cloud already carrying proof of identity.

The factory is a threat surface, not a safe zone. Devices are often built by contract manufacturers we don't fully control. The assembly line is exactly where keys are most exposed. A good provisioning design assumes the factory is semi-trusted and makes sure that even someone watching the factory network learns nothing useful.

The whole lifecycle (not just "issue a key")

  • Provision — mint a unique identity + keys at manufacture / first boot.
  • Attest — later, the device proves "I am genuine and un-tampered."
  • Protect — keys stay locked in hardware and never travel.
  • Revoke & renew — stop trusting a device whose key leaked; refresh expiring certs.
Interview angle

Weak answers stop at "generate a key and store it." Strong answers say the sentence: "per-device identity, signed by a hardware-protected authority, verifiable offline, with revocation designed in from day one — and I treat the factory as untrusted." Naming the full lifecycle (provision → attest → revoke) in the first minute signals seniority.

Per-device identity & the hardware root of trust

Analogy. Imagine a school issuing every student a locker with a key. Two ways to do it: give every student the same master key (easy, but if one copy leaks, every locker in the school is open), or give each student a unique key (a bit more bookkeeping, but a stolen key opens exactly one locker). Device identity works the same way — and the industry chose unique keys per device for a very good reason.

Why per-device keys, not one shared key

One shared key (per model)Per-device key (the modern default)
Blast radiusExtract it once → every unit in the world is forgeable.Extract it → exactly one unit is affected.
RevocationYou'd have to kill the whole product line.Revoke just that one device; everyone else is fine.
CostSimple, tiny registry.Needs on-device key generation + a registry of many identities.

Say this: "Per-device keys bound the blast radius to a single unit and make targeted revocation possible. A shared secret built into every unit is the classic mistake — one extraction breaks everything."

Hardware root of trust & the secure element (conceptual)

A secure element is a tiny, tamper-resistant vault built into the device — a chip (or a protected region of a chip) whose whole job is to generate and guard secret keys. Its superpower: the private key can be created inside the vault and never comes out. The device can ask the vault to "sign this" and get a signature back, but it can never read the raw key. Even someone who pries the device open and probes the circuit board is designed to come away with nothing.

This vault is the device's root of trust: the one small piece we anchor everything else on. If you trust that the private key was born inside genuine hardware and never escaped, then a valid signature from that key is convincing proof of "this specific genuine device."

Two secrets, protected differently. The device's private key lives in the device's secure element. The authority's signing key (next section) lives in the factory's HSM. Neither is ever transmitted over the wire — which is exactly why tapping the factory network is useless to an attacker.

We stay at the system/protocol layer here: what the secure element guarantees, not how the silicon fuses or firmware implement it. That's deliberate — the design reasoning doesn't need the transistor-level detail.

Interview angle

If asked "where does the key come from?", the best answer is "generated on-device inside a secure element, so the private key never exists anywhere else." Contrast it with injecting a key from outside (weaker, because the key exists off-device for a moment). A strong answer ties root of trust → never-exported private key → a signature = proof of genuine hardware.

Certificate issuance: a lightweight PKI story

Analogy. A passport is only useful because a government you already trust issued it. You don't personally verify every citizen — you trust the issuing authority, and the passport's anti-forgery features tie back to that authority. A certificate is a device's passport, and a Certificate Authority (CA) is the government that signs it.

What a certificate actually is

A certificate is just: the device's public key + some identity info (like a serial number), wrapped up and digitally signed by the CA. Anyone can check the CA's signature. If it's valid, they know the CA vouched for "this public key belongs to this device" — without contacting the CA at all.

# A per-device certificate, conceptually
certificate {
  subject:    "device A7F3, product = doorbell-v2"
  public_key: "04a9f1…"        # the device's PUBLIC half only
  issuer:     "Doorbell Product CA"
  valid:      2026-01-01 .. 2028-01-01
  signature:  "3045022…"        # the CA's signature over all of the above
}

The chain of trust (why we don't just have one CA)

Real systems use a tree, so a single compromise is contained:

LayerWhere it livesWhy
Root CAOffline, air-gapped, quorum-controlled (its key is split into shares so no single person can use it).Ultimate anchor. Kept offline so a network breach can't reach it.
Product / Intermediate CAOne per product line; key sealed in an HSM on the factory signing server.This is what actually signs devices. If a line is compromised, revoke one intermediate, not the root.
Per-device certUnique per unit; the device keeps the matching private key.Every device provable individually; one leaked key = one revoked device.

The magic of offline verification: because a certificate carries the CA's signature, any relying party can verify a device by checking the chain up to the root's public key it already has — without ever calling the root or the CA. That's what lets billions of verifications happen in the field cheaply. The one caveat: chain and signature validation is fully offline, but confirming a cert hasn't been revoked may need fresh status — which is exactly why the revocation section below leans on cached CRLs or short-lived certs as the offline-friendly answer.

Interview angle

Expect: "Why an intermediate CA and not just the root?" Answer: blast-radius and availability — the root stays offline and pristine, intermediates do the day-to-day signing and can be rotated or revoked per product line. Bonus point: "verification never contacts the root; it validates the chain locally."

The provisioning handshake — step by step

Analogy. A new employee getting a badge: they show up, prove who they are to the security desk, the desk checks with HR and prints a badge signed by the company, and HR records that the badge exists. The device version is almost identical — device ↔ provisioning service ↔ CA — and the key detail is that the device's secret never leaves its pocket. Only its public half travels.

Step 0 / 6
Press Step to begin. The device starts with no identity at all.

The steps in words

  1. Generate key pair on-device. The secure element creates a fresh private/public pair. The private key never leaves.
  2. Emit a CSR (Certificate Signing Request) — the public key + identity info, signed by the device itself to prove it holds the matching private key.
  3. Provisioning service validates & forwards. The line talks to the signing service over mutual TLS (both sides authenticate, because the line is only semi-trusted).
  4. CA signs the CSR using the product CA key inside the HSM. The key never comes out — only a signature does.
  5. Certificate chain returns to the provisioning service.
  6. Install & record. The cert is written to the device; the device's public identity is logged to a central registry so the cloud recognizes it later.

Factory realities. A line runs in seconds per unit, so signing is a synchronous call with immediate pass/fail — a bad unit must be flagged now. Buffer the registry upload if the network blips, and support an offline signing fallback (HSM on-prem) for air-gapped contract manufacturers, reconciling the log later.

Interview angle

Draw exactly these three actors and walk the arrows. The line that earns points: "Only the CSR — the public half — ever travels. The device private key and the CA private key each stay locked in their own hardware, so intercepting the factory network gets an attacker nothing." Mention synchronous signing + offline fallback and you've shown you understand real manufacturing.

Attestation — proving "I am genuine and un-tampered"

Analogy. Provisioning gives you the passport. Attestation is showing it at the border and giving a live signature so the officer knows the passport belongs to you, right now — not to someone who photocopied it. A photo of a passport isn't enough; you have to sign a fresh challenge on the spot.

The challenge–response, step by step

  1. The cloud (or any relying party) sends a random nonce — a fresh, one-time number.
  2. The device signs the nonce with its private key (inside the secure element).
  3. The relying party verifies the signature against the device's certificate, validates the cert chain up to the trusted root, and checks it isn't revoked.
# Why the random nonce matters
cloud  → device:  nonce = 0x9f3c…          # fresh each time
device → cloud:   sign(private_key, nonce)     # only the real device can do this
cloud:            verify(cert.public_key, nonce, signature)
                  && chain_valid(cert → root)
                  && not_revoked(cert)

Why a fresh nonce? If the challenge were fixed, an attacker could record one valid response and replay it forever. A random per-session nonce means a captured signature is worthless next time — the device must sign the new challenge live, which only the real private key can do.

Attesting software state, not just hardware

Stronger attestation proves not only "genuine hardware" but "running expected, un-tampered software." Conceptually, the device's identity/attestation can be bound to a measurement of its firmware (a hash of the code it booted). If the code was modified, the measurement changes and attestation reflects it. This is what lets a fleet operator say "only devices running an approved build may connect" — and it's the hook that pairs with Secure OTA Updates (Ch 10): after a patch, devices re-attest with the new measurement.

Say this: "Attestation = signature over a fresh nonce + chain validation + revocation check, optionally bound to a firmware measurement so it proves software integrity too — not just hardware genuineness."

Interview angle

The classic follow-up is "how do you stop replay?" — answer fresh nonce. The senior follow-up is "can you prove the device isn't running tampered code?" — answer bind a firmware measurement into the attestation, and connect it to remediation after an OTA update. That link across chapters is exactly the kind of systems thinking interviewers reward.

Where provisioning happens: injection vs zero-touch

Analogy. Two ways a new hire can get their laptop set up: IT images it fully at headquarters before shipping (everything built in up front), or the laptop arrives blank and configures itself securely the first time the employee logs in on the corporate network (set up "just in time"). Devices have the same two philosophies.

Factory injection (provision fully at manufacture)

The complete identity — keys and certificate — is created and installed on the line. When the device ships, it already has everything. This is the classic model and pairs naturally with on-device key generation described earlier.

Zero-touch / late binding (provision at first boot)

The factory installs only a minimal bootstrap credential — a birth token that says "I'm a blank genuine unit from this batch." The real per-device identity is issued later, the first time the device powers on and reaches the provisioning service (often over the customer's network). The device trades its bootstrap credential for its true operational certificate.

Factory injectionZero-touch / late binding
When identity is mintedOn the line, at manufacture.At first boot, in the field.
Factory trust neededHigher — full keys handled on the line.Lower — factory only installs a bootstrap token.
FlexibilityIdentity fixed early; binding to an owner/region is harder.Can bind device to its actual owner/account/region at activation.
ConnectivityWorks even if the line is air-gapped.Needs the device online at first boot.

Key idea: "late binding" defers the sensitive step out of the untrusted factory and into a moment you control better (first boot on a known network). The trade is that it depends on connectivity and a solid bootstrap-credential story.

On-device keygen vs injected keys (either model)

ApproachHowTrade-off
On-device keygen (preferred)Device's secure element makes the key pair; only the CSR leaves.Best security — private key never exists anywhere but the device.
Injected keysA trusted server generates the pair and loads it in.Works for constrained, low-capability devices, but the private key exists off-device for a moment — needs a very trusted, HSM-backed injection environment.
Interview angle

Naming zero-touch / late binding unprompted signals you've seen real IoT fleets. The crisp position: "on-device keygen whenever the hardware can do crypto; late binding to keep the sensitive step out of an untrusted factory; injection only for constrained devices in an HSM-backed environment." Give a pick, not just a list.

Key protection & revocation

Analogy. Two jobs: keep the master key-cutting machine in a bank vault (protect the CA key), and be able to cancel a lost badge without re-issuing everyone's badge (revocation). A security design that can't cancel trust is only half a design.

Protecting the CA key (the crown jewel)

  • Root: offline + quorum. Air-gapped; the key is split into shares so several admins must combine them to use it — not a vote, actual cryptographic shares.
  • Intermediate: HSM-resident, auth-gated. Used on the line but never exported; its access secret is delivered separately from the HSM.
  • Rotation: intermediates are rotated periodically so any undetected compromise has a bounded window.

Revocation — stop trusting a device, going forward

Assume some keys will leak (a device cloned, a contract manufacturer compromised). You need to stop trusting those without breaking everyone else.

MechanismHow it worksTrade-off
CRL (revocation list)A signed list of revoked IDs, distributed to relying parties.Simple; can grow large. Compress + CDN + cache. Not instant (cache TTL).
OCSP / status serviceAsk online: "is this cert still valid?"Fresher, but adds a network call & a dependency. Stapling avoids the per-verify call.
Short-lived certsCerts expire fast; devices renew often."Revocation by expiry" — a leaked key dies on its own soon. Needs a renewal path + connectivity.
Intermediate revocationRevoke a whole product-CA / batch at once.Blast-radius control: kill one compromised line without touching the root or other products.

The elegant framing: "Revocation is just 'exclude the bad ones going forward.' Whether it's a CRL, short-lived certs, or re-issuing so only good units get a fresh cert — the principle is identical." Store provenance (line, batch, date) in the registry so you can scope a recall precisely.

The registry stores public material only

  • Never store a private key centrally — only public certs + provenance. If the DB leaks, no identity can be forged.
  • Integrity > secrecy here — protect the registry against tampering (signed/hash-chained rows), because faking a row = faking a genuine device.
  • Keep owner/account mapping separate — don't co-locate the owner/account/region binding (PII) with the device-identity registry. Retain only the minimum provenance a recall actually needs, and remember that a persistent, unique per-device identifier is a tracking/linkability vector: it's a privacy trade-off to be managed, segregated, and minimized, not ignored.
Interview angle

Forgetting revocation is an easy way to fail this question. Bring it up unprompted. Then handle the classic tricky follow-ups: "a contract manufacturer is compromised" → revoke that line's intermediate CA, rotate, root untouched; "a single device key is extracted" → blast radius is one device, revoke that ID; "how do devices verify offline" → local chain validation + a cached CRL or short-lived certs so staleness self-heals.

The through-line: mint a hardware-rooted identity once — ideally where you control trust most (on-device keygen, late binding out of an untrusted factory) — keep every private key where it's born, let anyone verify it offline against a trusted root, and design revocation in from the start so a compromise is always contained.
