Design a Payment System

The system-design problem where correctness beats every other quality: money must never be lost, never created out of thin air, and never charged twice. We build up from a high-schooler's "charge a card" API to a senior engineer's idempotent, double-entry, reconciled money mover — every invariant stated precisely and every number verified so you can quote it in an interview. Card handling is kept generic (tokenize, never store the raw number) — no real processor internals.
Read this like a story

Start at the top. Payments look like "call an API and move money," but the entire chapter exists to answer two questions an interviewer often presses on: how do you never double-charge (idempotency, §4) and how do you never lose or invent money (the double-entry ledger, §5). Everything else — sagas, reconciliation, PCI — hangs off those two invariants.

1. The Problem & Requirements

A payment system moves money on behalf of a business: it charges a customer, refunds them when needed, and pays out to a merchant or seller. It sits between the customer's funding source (a card, a bank account, a wallet) and the parties who ultimately receive the money, orchestrating external processors while keeping its own perfectly accurate books. If a rate limiter can drop a request with no lasting harm, a payment system cannot: a lost or duplicated write here is real money.

What "correctness above all" actually means

Before any diagram, internalize the non-negotiable invariants — the interviewer is listening for these words:

  • No money is ever lost. Every cent that enters the system is accounted for somewhere; nothing silently disappears on a crash or timeout.
  • No money is ever created. You cannot invent a balance; every credit has a matching debit (the double-entry law in §5).
  • No customer is ever double-charged. A client retry, a network timeout, or a duplicate webhook must never result in two charges (idempotency, §4).
  • Every movement is auditable. Given any balance, you can reconstruct exactly which events produced it — an immutable, append-only history (§5).

Scope: charge, refund, payout

We keep the surface deliberately small so we can go deep on correctness. Three flows cover most of the interview:

In scope

  • Charge — collect money from a customer's funding source (authorize + capture).
  • Refund — return money for a prior capture, fully or partially.
  • Payout — move settled funds out to a merchant/seller bank account.
  • Ledger & balances — the internal books that record all of the above.

Explicitly out of scope

  • Storing raw card data (PAN). We tokenize via a vault/PSP and keep the sensitive data out of our systems entirely (§8).
  • Fraud/risk scoring, chargeback dispute UX, tax, FX pricing — mention them, don't build them.
  • The card network's internal clearing — we treat the PSP / acquiring bank as an external dependency.

Functional vs. non-functional requirements

Functional

  • Create a charge for an amount + currency against a tokenized funding source.
  • Support the authorize → capture → settle lifecycle (§3), plus void and refund.
  • Every request carries a client-supplied idempotency key; a retry returns the original result.
  • Record every movement in a double-entry ledger and expose derived balances.
  • Reconcile internal records against processor/bank statements (§7).

Non-functional

  • Strong consistency for the ledger. Balances and journal writes are transactional and exact — no eventual-consistency guesses on money.
  • Auditability & immutability. The journal is append-only; you correct by appending a reversal, never by editing.
  • Idempotency & exactly-once effect on every mutating call.
  • High availability for the charge path (revenue), but correctness wins ties — prefer failing a charge to double-charging.
  • Security & compliance — tokenization, least privilege, and a tamper-evident audit log (§8).
The one-liner that sets you apart

"For most systems I'd trade consistency for availability; for the ledger I do the opposite — it is strongly consistent and append-only, and I'd rather reject a payment than record it twice. Availability I recover with idempotent retries, not by relaxing correctness." Saying this in the first two minutes signals seniority.

2. Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

Sizing tells you whether one relational primary suffices for the ledger or you must shard, and how much the append-only history costs to retain for compliance.

Transactions per day and peak QPS

Assume a mid-size platform: 10,000,000 payment transactions/day (charges, refunds, payouts combined).

txns/day = 10,000,000 (107)
seconds/day = 86,400 ≈ 105
average QPS = 107 / 86,400 ≈ 116 QPS # ≈100 with the ÷10⁵ shortcut
peak QPS = average × (peak factor ~10, e.g. a flash sale) ≈ ~1,160 QPS

Writes are modest — ~100–1,200 QPS — which is tiny for a database. That is deliberate: payments are write-light but correctness-heavy. You are not fighting throughput; you are fighting duplicates, partial failures, and lost updates. Reads (status checks, balance lookups, dashboards) run perhaps 10× the writes and are served from read replicas / read models (§10).

Ledger write volume

The load that actually matters is ledger postings, because double-entry multiplies each business transaction into several journal lines. A single capture is ~3 postings (see the worked example in §5); averaged across the charge/refund/payout flows — some of which add reversal and settlement lines — budget ~4 postings per transaction.

postings/day = 107 txns × 4 lines = 40,000,000 ledger rows/day
postings/sec (avg) = 4 × 116 ≈ ~464 rows/sec (peak ~4,600/sec)

Storage & retention

Each ledger row stores account ids, a signed amount, currency, a transaction id, references, and timestamps — call it ~300 bytes once indexed. Financial records are commonly retained for ~7 years for compliance.

per day = 40,000,000 × 300 B = 12,000,000,000 B = 12 GB/day
7 years ≈ 7 × 365 = 2,555 days
total = 12 GB × 2,555 ≈ 30,660 GB ≈ ~30 TB # raw journal, pre-compression
Takeaway

~30 TB over seven years is comfortably in single-cluster relational territory (with partitioning by time/account and cold-tiering old years to cheaper storage). The scaling story here is durability, integrity, and retention — not QPS. Lead with that and you have framed the problem correctly.

3. Core Flow & Entities

Before optimizing, nail the vocabulary. Interviewers reward precise terms, because sloppy naming here is where double-charges hide.

The core entities

OrderThe business intent: "customer C owes $100 for cart X."Belongs to the product domain; a payment is attached to it.
Payment intentA durable object representing "we intend to collect $100 from this funding source," created before we touch any processor.Anchors the idempotency key and the state machine; one intent, many attempts.

Intent vs. attempt — the key distinction

The payment intent is created and persisted first, keyed by the client's idempotency key. Every retry, timeout recovery, or webhook re-delivery maps back to the same intent and produces (at most) additional attempts. This one modeling choice is what makes "at-most-one successful charge per intent" enforceable.

The authorize → capture → settle lifecycle

Card payments are not a single atomic "charge." They are a two-step hold-then-collect, followed by out-of-band bank settlement. Knowing these three words cold is a core signal:

  • Authorize — ask the bank to place a hold on the funds and confirm they exist. No money moves yet; the hold can expire or be voided.
  • Capture — tell the bank to actually collect the authorized amount (often at ship time). This is when we owe the merchant and recognize the movement in our ledger.
  • Settle — the bank/network transfers real funds into the acquiring account, typically hours-to-days later, in batches. This is asynchronous and is what §7 reconciles against.

The charge API

A minimal, idempotent charge endpoint. The Idempotency-Key header is mandatory — it is the heart of §4.

# Request — the key is client-generated and stable across retries
POST /v1/charges
Idempotency-Key: "a1b2c3-d4e5-6789"
{
  "amount": 10000,            # minor units (cents) — NEVER floats for money
  "currency": "usd",
  "source_token": "tok_9f...",  # tokenized card from the vault/PSP — no raw PAN
  "order_id": "ord_5521",
  "capture": true            # true = auth+capture now; false = auth only
}

# Response — the same body is returned for the original and every retry
HTTP/1.1 201 Created
{
  "id": "pi_88a2",
  "status": "captured",      # see the state machine in §6
  "amount": 10000,
  "currency": "usd"
}
Money is integers, never floats

Store amounts in minor units (cents) as integers, or a fixed-precision decimal. Binary floating point cannot represent 0.10 exactly, so 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3 — an unacceptable rounding error when it compounds across millions of postings. Every serious ledger uses integer minor units or arbitrary-precision decimals. Mentioning this unprompted is an easy senior signal.

4. Idempotency — Never Double-Charge

This is the crux of the whole problem. The network is unreliable: a client sends POST /charges, the charge succeeds, but the response is lost to a timeout. The client, seeing no answer, retries. Without protection, that is a second, real charge. Idempotency guarantees that a retried request produces exactly one effect and returns the same result as the first.

The mechanism: a client key mapped to a stored result

The client generates a unique idempotency key for each logical operation (not each network attempt) and sends it on every retry of that operation. The server keeps a durable idempotency store: key → (request fingerprint, response, status). On each request it does an atomic check:

First call: MISS → reserve key, charge once, persist the result. Any retry with the same key K: HIT → return the stored result without charging again.

The critical ordering rules

Idempotency is only correct if you get the ordering and atomicity right. The subtle bugs live here:

# Idempotent charge — the ordering is what makes it safe
def charge(key, request):
    # 1. Atomically reserve the key BEFORE doing external work.
    #    A unique index on `key` makes a concurrent duplicate fail here.
    row = insert_if_absent(key, status="in_progress",
                          fingerprint=hash(request))

    if row.existed:                       # key seen before
        if row.fingerprint != hash(request):
            return 422                    # SAME key, DIFFERENT body → reject
        if row.status == "in_progress":
            return 409                    # a concurrent original is still running
        return row.stored_response         # HIT → replay the exact result

    # 2. First time only: do the real work, keyed so the PSP is idempotent too.
    result = psp.charge(request, idempotency_key=key)

    # 3. Persist ledger entry AND the stored response in ONE transaction.
    with db.transaction():
        post_journal_entry(result)       # double-entry write (§5)
        save_response(key, result, status="done")
    return result
The three classic idempotency bugs

(1) Reserving the key after the external call — two concurrent requests both miss, both charge. Fix: insert the key first, under a unique constraint. (2) Not binding the key to the request body — a client reuses a key for a different amount and gets the wrong cached answer. Fix: store a fingerprint and reject mismatches (422). (3) Persisting the ledger write and the stored response in separate transactions — a crash between them leaves you charged with no recorded result, so the retry charges again. Fix: one atomic transaction.

Push idempotency all the way down

Your service being idempotent is not enough if the PSP is not. Pass the same key (or a derived one) to the processor so their side also dedups — most payment processors accept an idempotency key exactly for this. The chain "client key → your service → PSP key" is what makes the whole path exactly-once in effect, even though the network delivers at-least-once.

"Every mutating call takes a client idempotency key. I reserve it under a unique index before any external work, bind it to a hash of the request body, and persist the ledger entry and the stored response in a single transaction. Retries replay the stored result, and I forward the key to the PSP so the processor dedups too — so at-least-once delivery becomes exactly-once effect."

5. The Double-Entry Ledger

The ledger is the system's source of truth for money. It uses double-entry accounting, a 700-year-old discipline that makes "money lost or invented" structurally impossible: every movement is recorded as equal and opposite debits and credits across accounts. Money is never destroyed — only moved from one account to another.

The invariant: sum(debits) = sum(credits)

A journal entry is an atomic set of postings (lines). Its ironclad rule:

for every journal entry: Σ debits = Σ credits
# and therefore, summed across the whole immutable journal:
Σ all debits = Σ all credits # the books always balance — checkable at any time

If a proposed entry does not balance, it is rejected before it is written — a bug can never even persist an unbalanced state. This single constraint is why double-entry is so powerful here: it turns "don't lose money" from a hope into an enforced invariant you can assert on every write and audit continuously.

A balanced worked example: a $100 capture with a $3 fee

A customer pays $100.00. The processor will owe us that $100; we will owe the merchant $97.00 and keep $3.00 as fee revenue. One balanced journal entry records it — note debits and credits each total exactly 100.00:

One balanced journal entry for a $100 capture: a $100 debit to Processor Receivable, offset by $97 owed to the merchant and $3 of fee revenue. Debits equal credits, so the entry is valid.

A refund is simply the reverse entry — debit Merchant Payable and Fee Revenue, credit Processor Receivable (reducing the asset) — again balanced. You never delete the original; you append a compensating entry, preserving the full audit trail.

Deriving balances from the journal

An account's balance is not a field you edit — it is a derived quantity: the sum of its postings.

balance(account) = Σ debits(account) − Σ credits(account) # sign convention per account type

Because balances are derived, they can always be recomputed from first principles and checked against any cached value. For performance you keep a materialized balance (a running total, or periodic snapshots you sum forward from), but that cache is an optimization — the journal remains the truth.

Why not just mutate a balance column?

The tempting shortcut is a single accounts(balance) row you UPDATE on each payment. It is wrong for money:

Mutable balance column — why it fails
  • No history: you can't answer "why is the balance this?" or audit a dispute.
  • Lost updates: concurrent UPDATEs race; a crash mid-update corrupts the number with no way to detect it.
  • No cross-check: a wrong value is indistinguishable from a right one — nothing to reconcile against.
  • Can silently violate conservation (money appears/vanishes).
Append-only double-entry — why it wins
  • Immutable history: every balance is fully reconstructable and auditable.
  • The Σdebits = Σcredits invariant is checkable on every write and continuously.
  • Corrections are reversing entries, not edits — the trail is preserved.
  • Reconciliation (§7) has something concrete to compare against.
The interview soundbite

"I don't store a mutable balance; I store an immutable, append-only journal of balanced double-entry postings and derive balances by summing. Corrections are reversing entries. That gives me a provable debits = credits invariant, a full audit trail, and something to reconcile against — none of which a mutable balance column can offer."

6. Consistency & Distributed Transactions

Real payments touch several services — a payment service, a ledger, a wallet, a notifier — and external processors. Coordinating a money movement across them, correctly, under partial failure, is the hard distributed-systems core of this problem.

Exactly-once is a goal, achieved via idempotency + dedup

State it plainly, because interviewers push on it: true exactly-once delivery is impossible over an unreliable network — you cannot guarantee a message is delivered once and only once. What you engineer instead is exactly-once effect: messages are delivered at-least-once, and idempotency + deduplication (§4) collapse the duplicates so the observable result is as if each operation happened exactly once. "Exactly-once" is an outcome you build, not a transport you buy.

Saga vs. 2PC for multi-service money movement

Two ways to make a change span multiple services atomically-ish:

2PC (left): the coordinator runs two phases — ① PREPARE and ② COMMIT — and each phase is sent to all participants; participants vote/ack back and hold locks, blocking until the decision (all vote yes → commit, anyone fails → all abort). Saga (right): each step commits locally, and failures trigger compensating transactions run in reverse order.
2PC (two-phase commit)
  • Strong atomicity across services in one logical commit.
  • Blocking: participants hold locks through the prepare phase; a slow/dead coordinator stalls everyone.
  • Coordinator is a failure point; poor availability and latency at scale.
  • Rarely used across service/PSP boundaries in modern payments.
Saga (compensating transactions)
  • Each step is a local transaction; no distributed locks, no blocking.
  • Failure is handled by running compensations in reverse (release hold, post reversal).
  • Eventually consistent, highly available, partial-failure resilient.
  • The pragmatic default for multi-service money movement — compensations must be idempotent.
Compensation isn't a rollback

A saga cannot "undo" a committed local step — it can only compensate with a new, semantically-reversing action (a captured charge is compensated by a refund, not by pretending it never happened). Because a compensation may itself be retried, it must be idempotent, and some effects (an email already sent) are not reversible — design steps so the irreversible ones come last.

The outbox pattern for reliable events

Sagas and downstream consumers depend on events ("payment captured"). But writing to your database and publishing to a message broker are two systems — a crash between them means the ledger records a capture that the rest of the system never hears about (or a published event for a write that rolled back). The transactional outbox fixes this:

  • In the same local transaction as the ledger write, insert the event into an outbox table. Either both commit or neither does.
  • A separate relay polls the outbox (or tails the DB change log) and publishes to the broker, marking rows sent.
  • Delivery is at-least-once, so consumers dedup by event id — exactly-once effect again.
Why it matters here

The outbox guarantees "if the ledger recorded it, the event will eventually be published, and if it rolled back, no event escapes." That atomic coupling of state change and event is what keeps a saga from getting stuck half-done — the single most common source of stuck/duplicated payments without it.

The payment state machine

A payment intent moves through a small set of states with only legal transitions. Enforcing this machine — rejecting any illegal transition (e.g. capturing something already refunded) — is a primary correctness guard.

Legal transitions only: pending→authorized→captured→settled along the top; pending→failed, authorized→voided, and captured/settled→refunded downward. Any other transition (e.g. capture after refund) is rejected.
Illegal transitions are money bugs

Guard the machine in the database (a status column with a check, or a transition table), not just in app code. "Capture an already-refunded intent," "refund more than was captured," or "authorize twice" are exactly the paths that create phantom money — and they are trivial to block with an explicit legal-transition check plus the amount constraints (refunded ≤ captured).

7. Reconciliation

Your ledger is your version of the truth. The PSP, the card network, and the bank each keep their own. Because settlement is asynchronous and batched, and because messages get lost, these versions drift. Reconciliation is the periodic process that compares your internal ledger against external statements, detects discrepancies, and repairs them — the safety net that catches whatever slipped past idempotency and sagas.

The reconciliation loop

  1. Ingest external statements. Pull the processor/bank settlement report for a period (a daily file or API export of every movement they recorded).
  2. Match. Join external records to internal journal entries on a shared key (a transaction reference the PSP echoes back). Most will match exactly.
  3. Classify discrepancies. The mismatches fall into a few buckets (below).
  4. Repair. Append correcting journal entries (never edit history) and/or re-drive the stuck operation with its original idempotency key so the retry is safe.

The discrepancy types

CaseWhat it meansRepair
In ledger, not in statementWe recorded a capture the processor has no record of (or it hasn't settled yet).If just timing → wait for the next batch. If truly missing → investigate; re-drive with the same idempotency key, or reverse.
In statement, not in ledgerThe processor moved money we never recorded (e.g. a webhook we missed).Append the missing journal entry from the authoritative statement; ingest the dropped event.
Amount mismatchBoth sides have it, but the amounts differ (fee applied differently, partial capture).Append an adjusting entry for the delta; flag for review if unexplained.
Status mismatchWe think "captured," they show "refunded/failed."Reconcile the state machine to the external source of truth; append the reversing entry.

Eventual settlement and safe retries

Settlement lands hours-to-days after capture, so a "missing" record is usually just not settled yet — reconciliation must tolerate timing lag and only escalate after a grace window. When it does re-drive a stuck operation, it reuses the original idempotency key (§4): re-driving is safe precisely because a duplicate collapses to a no-op. Idempotency and reconciliation are complementary — idempotency prevents duplicates in the moment; reconciliation catches the residue and repairs it after the fact.

Why interviewers ask about it

Reconciliation is the honest admission that at scale, something always drifts. A candidate who says "idempotency guarantees perfection" is naive; a candidate who adds "…and I reconcile against the processor/bank daily, classify the discrepancies, and repair with appended entries and idempotent re-drives" is describing how real money systems actually stay correct.

8. Security & Compliance (PCI, generically)

Handling card data brings compliance obligations (the PCI-DSS family of standards). The single most powerful design move is to keep sensitive card data out of your systems entirely — you cannot leak what you never hold. Everything below is generic best practice; no real processor internals.

Tokenize — never store the raw card number (PAN)

  • The customer's card number (the PAN, primary account number) is captured directly by a vault / PSP — often via a hosted field or SDK so the raw digits never transit your servers.
  • The vault returns an opaque token; your systems store and pass only the token. A token is useless if stolen — it cannot be turned back into a card number outside the vault.
  • This pulls the bulk of your infrastructure out of PCI scope: the sensitive data lives in the vault's certified environment, drastically shrinking what you must audit and secure.
Never do this

Do not log, cache, store in analytics, or pass through your own databases a raw PAN, CVV, or full track data. The CVV in particular must never be stored after authorization. If a raw card number ever lands in your logs, you have a compliance incident and a breach waiting to happen. The token is the only card reference your code should ever see.

Least privilege and defense in depth

  • Least privilege: the ledger-writing service can append journal entries but cannot read tokens; the charge service can request a charge with a token but cannot read the ledger's raw tables. Scope every credential to the minimum it needs.
  • Encryption everywhere: TLS in transit; encryption at rest for any stored data; secrets in a managed secret store, rotated regularly.
  • Segmentation: the payment path runs in its own isolated environment with tight network controls, separate from general application services.

The audit log

Every sensitive action — a charge, a refund, a config change, an access to a token — writes to an append-only, tamper-evident audit log (who, what, when, from where). Combined with the immutable ledger, this gives you the two records auditors and incident responders need: what money moved (ledger) and who did what (audit log). Both are append-only for the same reason — history you can edit is history you cannot trust.

"I keep card data out of scope: the PAN is captured straight into a vault/PSP that returns a token, and my services only ever handle the token — I never store the raw number or the CVV. Add least-privilege credentials per service, encryption in transit and at rest, and an append-only audit log alongside the immutable ledger, and most of my infrastructure falls outside PCI scope."

9. Scaling & Failure Handling

Payments are write-light (§2), so scaling is less about raw QPS and more about partitioning the ledger without breaking its invariants and handling partial failure without losing or duplicating money.

Sharding the ledger by account

  • Shard by account id (or customer/merchant id). Most journal entries touch accounts that hash to the same shard, so they stay within a single-shard transaction where the debits = credits invariant is trivially enforced.
  • Cross-shard entries (a payout moving money between accounts on different shards) become a mini-saga or a 2PC across shards — kept rare by co-locating related accounts.
  • Reads (balances, dashboards) scale on read replicas and read models (§10); the write primary stays authoritative and strongly consistent.

Hot accounts

The hot-account bottleneck

A few accounts are enormous write hotspots — a platform-wide fee revenue or cash account touched by every transaction. Serializing all writes on one row throttles the whole system. Mitigations: sub-account sharding (split the hot account into N shadow accounts that sum to the real balance), batching/aggregating postings to the hot account periodically instead of per-transaction, and keeping per-customer accounts (which are naturally spread) on the hot path.

Partial failures: the "authorized but capture failed" case

The canonical payment failure: you successfully authorized (funds are held on the customer's card) but the capture call failed or timed out. Now what?

  • You genuinely don't know whether the capture succeeded — a timeout is ambiguous. Never assume; query the PSP for the definitive status, or safely retry the capture with the same idempotency key so a duplicate collapses to a no-op.
  • If the capture truly failed and won't be retried, void the authorization so the hold is released and the customer isn't left with money frozen.
  • Reconciliation (§7) is the backstop: a dangling auth with no matching capture/void surfaces in the daily match and gets resolved.

Timeouts + idempotent retry — the universal pattern

Every external money call follows the same discipline: timeout → don't assume → retry with the original idempotency key (with exponential backoff + jitter). Because the key makes the retry a no-op if the first call actually landed, you can retry aggressively without fear of double-charging. This is the operational payoff of §4 — idempotency is what lets you retry at all in a money system.

A timeout is not a failure

The most dangerous assumption in payments is "the call timed out, so it didn't happen." A timeout means unknown — the charge may well have succeeded and only the response was lost. Treating unknown as failure (and re-charging) is exactly how double-charges happen. Resolve unknowns by querying status or retrying idempotently — never by guessing.

The unifying principle: apply strong consistency to the ledger write path (money) and accept eventual consistency on the read/derived side (balances, dashboards, notifications). Knowing exactly where to draw that line — strict on the journal, relaxed on read models — is the senior judgment call this problem tests.

How to drive the conversation

  1. Clarify (1–2 min): Which flows — charge, refund, payout? Custodial (we hold funds) or pass-through? Single currency? Who's the funding source (card via a PSP)? State that correctness > availability up front.
  2. Estimate (§2): txns/day → QPS (write-light), then ledger postings and 7-year retention → the real story is durability, not throughput.
  3. Model the entities & lifecycle (§3): intent vs. attempt, authorize→capture→settle, integer minor units.
  4. Lead with the two invariants: idempotency (§4) so you never double-charge, and the double-entry ledger (§5) so you never lose/invent money.
  5. Go distributed (§6): exactly-once effect via idempotency+dedup, saga over 2PC, outbox for reliable events, an enforced state machine.
  6. Close the loop (§7–§9): reconciliation against the processor/bank, tokenization/PCI, sharding + hot accounts, and the "authorized-but-capture-failed" timeout-retry story.

What interviewers probe (with one-liners)

Q: How do you avoid double-charging on a retry?
A client idempotency key per logical operation. I reserve it under a unique index before any external call, bind it to a hash of the request body, and persist the ledger write and the stored response in one transaction. Retries replay the stored result, and I forward the key to the PSP so it dedups too.

Q: What if money moved but a downstream step failed?
That's a saga: each step commits locally and failures run compensating transactions in reverse (a captured charge is compensated by a refund, not an "undo"). I make compensations idempotent, order irreversible steps last, and use a transactional outbox so the state change and its event commit atomically — no half-done, stuck payments.

Q: Why double-entry instead of a balance column?
Because it makes "money lost or invented" impossible: every entry has debits = credits, checkable on every write. The journal is immutable and append-only, so balances are derived and fully auditable, corrections are reversing entries, and I have something concrete to reconcile against. A mutable balance loses history and races.

Q: Can you really guarantee exactly-once?
Not exactly-once delivery — that's impossible over an unreliable network. I guarantee exactly-once effect: messages are at-least-once, and idempotency + dedup (keyed operations, event ids) collapse duplicates so the observable result is as if each op ran once.

Q: You authorized but the capture call timed out — now what?
A timeout is unknown, not failed. I query the PSP for the definitive status or retry the capture with the original idempotency key (a no-op if it already landed). If it truly failed, I void the auth to release the hold. Reconciliation catches any dangling auth.

Q: How do you keep the ledger correct while sharding it?
Shard by account so most entries stay single-shard, where debits = credits is a local transaction. Rare cross-shard entries become a mini-saga. Hot accounts (fee/cash) get sub-account sharding or batched postings so one row isn't a global bottleneck.

Q: How do you keep card data safe / in PCI scope control?
Tokenize: the PAN goes straight into a vault/PSP that returns a token; my services only ever handle the token, never the raw number or CVV. Add least-privilege credentials, encryption in transit and at rest, and an append-only audit log — which pulls most infrastructure out of PCI scope.

One-line summary

A payment system is a correctness machine: an immutable double-entry ledger (debits = credits, balances derived, never mutated) so money is never lost or invented, guarded by client idempotency keys so a retry never double-charges, coordinated across services by sagas + an outbox for exactly-once effect, kept honest by reconciliation against the processor/bank, and kept safe by tokenizing cards out of scope. Strong consistency on the money, eventual on the read models — and when in doubt, a timeout is unknown, so retry idempotently, never guess.

Terminology & Acronyms

Quick reference for the key terms in this chapter.

TermMeaning
PSPPayment Service Provider — the external processor that authorizes, captures, and settles card payments on your behalf.
Acquiring bankThe bank that receives settled funds into a merchant account; treated here as an external dependency behind the PSP.
PANPrimary Account Number — the raw card number; kept out of your systems entirely via tokenization.
CVVCard Verification Value — the card security code; must never be stored after authorization.
TokenizationReplacing a raw card number with an opaque token issued by a vault/PSP; the token is useless if stolen.
Idempotency keyA client-generated key per logical operation so that retries produce exactly one effect and replay the same result.
QPSQueries per second — the request-rate unit used for back-of-the-envelope sizing.
Payment intentA durable object representing the intent to collect a specific amount; anchors the idempotency key and state machine.
Payment attemptOne try at moving money via the PSP; retries create new attempts under the same intent.
AuthorizeAsk the bank to place a hold confirming funds exist; no money moves and the hold can expire or be voided.
CaptureInstruct the bank to actually collect the authorized amount; when the ledger records the movement.
SettleThe asynchronous, batched transfer of real funds into the acquiring account, typically hours to days later.
VoidCancel an authorization before capture, releasing the hold so funds are no longer frozen.
Double-entry ledgerAn append-only journal where every movement is recorded as equal debits and credits, so money is never lost or invented.
Debit / CreditThe two equal-and-opposite sides of every posting; within each journal entry, debits must equal credits.
Journal entryAn atomic, balanced set of postings recording one money movement; the immutable unit of truth.
SagaA multi-service transaction of local commits where failures are undone by compensating actions in reverse order.
2PCTwo-phase commit — a coordinator-driven prepare-then-commit protocol giving strong atomicity but blocking on held locks.
Compensating transactionA new, semantically-reversing action that undoes a committed saga step (e.g. a refund undoes a capture); must be idempotent.
Transactional outboxWriting an event into an outbox table in the same transaction as the state change, then relaying it to a broker.
Exactly-once effectThe observable result of at-least-once delivery plus idempotency and dedup — as if each operation ran exactly once.
ReconciliationThe periodic process that compares your ledger against processor/bank statements, classifies drift, and repairs it.
PCI-DSSPayment Card Industry Data Security Standard — the compliance family governing how card data must be handled.
