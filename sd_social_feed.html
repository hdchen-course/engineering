Design a Social Feed (News Feed)

The canonical read-heavy system-design problem: how do you build the "home timeline" that shows a user the latest posts from everyone they follow — starting from a version a high-schooler can reason about, up to the fan-out and celebrity problems that decide the design at scale. Every number and formula below is worked out so you can quote it in an interview.
Read this like a story

Start at the top even if you know the buzzwords. The chapter builds from "what even is a feed" to "how do you serve a 100-million-follower account without overwhelming the write path." The whole design hinges on one decision — pull vs. push vs. hybrid — so §4 is the crux; everything else supports it.

1. The Problem & Requirements

A social feed (a.k.a. news feed or home timeline) is the scrolling list of recent posts from all the accounts a user follows. You post; your followers see it in their feed. Think of it as a personalized, constantly-updating mailbox: every account you follow drops mail in, and opening the app shows you the most recent stack.

Three verbs define the whole system:

  • Post — a user publishes a piece of content (text, maybe an image/video link).
  • Follow — a user subscribes to another user's posts. This builds a directed follow graph (I follow you ≠ you follow me).
  • Read the home timeline — a user opens the app and gets a merged, time-ordered (or ranked) list of recent posts from everyone they follow.

Functional requirements

  • A user can publish a post, and it becomes visible to their followers.
  • A user can follow / unfollow another user.
  • A user can fetch their home timeline — recent posts from followees, newest-first, paginated as they scroll.
  • (Often also) fetch a single user's own profile timeline — just that one author's posts.

Non-functional requirements

The defining properties

  • Read-heavy. People scroll far more than they post — reads outnumber writes by roughly an order of magnitude or more (we quantify this in §2). Optimize the read path first.
  • Low-latency feed. Opening the app should return a feed in tens of milliseconds — precompute where you can so a read isn't a giant fan-in query.
  • Eventual consistency is OK. If your post reaches a follower's feed 1–2 seconds late, nobody notices or cares. This slack is what makes the whole design manageable.

Also state

  • High availability. The feed must render even if a piece is degraded — better to show a slightly stale feed than an error page.
  • Scale. Hundreds of millions of users, a highly skewed follower distribution (most have hundreds; a few have 100M+).
  • Out of scope (say so): exact ranking ML, ads, spam/abuse, media transcoding/CDN — mention them, then set them aside to focus on feed generation.
The one insight that shapes everything

Because it is read-heavy and eventual consistency is acceptable, we are free to do expensive work at write time (when someone posts) to make reads cheap. That trade — spend on the rare write to make the frequent read fast — is the heart of the feed design and the reason "fan-out on write" exists.

2. Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

Sizing tells you the read:write ratio (which justifies precomputing), the fan-out write load (the real bottleneck), and how much storage timelines cost. We'll carry these round numbers through the rest of the chapter.

Assumptions

QuantityAssumed valueWhy this rough estimate
Daily active users (DAU)200,000,000 (2×108)Large global social app
Avg posts per user per day0.5Most people read; few post daily
Avg feed opens per user per day10People check repeatedly
Avg followers per user200Typical; distribution is heavily skewed

Writes (posts/day) and reads (feed fetches/day)

posts/day = 200,000,000 × 0.5 = 100,000,000 (108 posts/day)
feed reads/day = 200,000,000 × 10 = 2,000,000,000 (2×109 reads/day)
read : write = 2,000M : 100M = 20 : 1 # decisively read-heavy → precompute reads

QPS (average and peak)

seconds/day = 86,400 ≈ 105
write QPS (posts) = 100,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 1,160 QPS → peak ×2 ≈ ~2,300 QPS
read QPS (feeds) = 2,000,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 23,000 QPS → peak ×2 ≈ ~46,000 QPS

Reads dominate by ~20×. A ~2,300-post/sec write path is small; a ~46,000-feed/sec read path is what we must make cheap. That asymmetry is the whole argument for doing work at write time.

Fan-out write amplification — the number that decides the design

If we precompute feeds by copying each new post into every follower's timeline (the "push" model, §4), one post becomes N writes, where N = the author's follower count. Averaged over all posts:

timeline writes/day = posts/day × avg followers
                 = 100,000,000 × 200 = 20,000,000,000 (2×1010 writes/day)
fan-out write QPS = 20,000,000,000 / 86,400 ≈ 231,000 QPS → peak ×2 ≈ ~460,000 QPS

write amplification = avg followers = 200× # one post → ~200 timeline inserts on average
This is the bottleneck, not the posts

The raw post rate is ~1,160/sec, but push turns it into ~231,000 timeline inserts/sec — a 200× amplification driven by the average follower count. And that's just the average; a single celebrity post amplifies by their follower count (millions). Controlling this amplification is exactly what the hybrid model in §4 exists to do.

Storage

Posts (source of truth). A post row is small: an 8-byte id, author id, up to ~280 characters of text, a timestamp, and counters — call it ~300 bytes raw, round to ~1 KB once you include DB row and index overhead.

post storage/day = 100,000,000 × 1 KB = 100 GB/day
post storage/year = 100 GB × 365 ≈ 36.5 TB/year # easily sharded across a cluster

Precomputed home timelines (the push cache). Each timeline entry stores just a reference — post_id (8 B) + author_id (8 B) + a rank score (4 B) ≈ ~20 bytes, not the post body. Cap each user's cached home timeline at the most recent 800 entries (nobody scrolls further; older pages fall back to the DB):

per user = 800 entries × 20 B = 16 KB
all users = 200,000,000 × 16 KB = 3.2 TB # fits an in-memory timeline cache cluster
Takeaway

Post bodies are ~36 TB/year (cheap disk, sharded). The precomputed timelines are only ~3.2 TB because they hold ID references, not content, and are capped per user — so they fit an in-memory store. The scary number isn't storage; it's the ~231k/s (peak ~460k/s) fan-out write rate.

3. Core Entities & APIs

Data model

Four entities. Note the follow graph is stored as edges you can traverse in both directions — you need "who follows me" (to fan out a post) and "who do I follow" (to build a feed by pulling).

EntityKey fieldsNotes
Useruser_id, handle, display name, follower_countfollower_count is the field the hybrid router reads to pick push vs. pull
Follow edge(follower_id, followee_id), created_atIndexed both ways: by follower (my followees) and by followee (my followers)
Postpost_id, author_id, text, media_ref, created_atSource of truth; post_id is time-sortable (e.g. Snowflake-style)
Home timelineuser_id → ordered list of post_idThe precomputed per-user feed cache (push model); capped & newest-first
Use time-sortable IDs

Make post_id encode its creation time (a Snowflake-style 64-bit id = timestamp bits + machine + sequence). Then "newest first" is just sorting by id, and cursor pagination (§5) needs no separate timestamp column. This is a small decision that simplifies ordering everywhere.

The three APIs

# 1. Publish a post
POST /v1/posts
  body: { text, media_ref? }
  # server sets author_id from auth, assigns time-sortable post_id
  → 201 { post_id, created_at }

# 2. Follow / unfollow
POST   /v1/users/{followee_id}/follow     → 204
DELETE /v1/users/{followee_id}/follow     → 204

# 3. Get the home timeline — CURSOR paginated, never offset (see §5)
GET /v1/feed?limit=20&cursor=<opaque>
  → 200 {
      items:       [ {post_id, author, text, created_at, ...}, ... ],
      next_cursor: "<opaque token = id of last item>"   # null when no more
    }

The client scrolls by sending back next_cursor; the server returns the next page of items strictly older than that cursor. No page numbers, no offset= — §5 explains why that matters on a live, growing feed.

4. Feed Generation — the Crux

Everything so far was setup. The interview turns on one question: when do we do the work of merging posts into a feed — at read time or at write time? There are two pure strategies and one hybrid that combines them. This is the section to get right.

4.1 Pull model (fan-out on read)

Do nothing special when a user posts — just store the post. Build the feed lazily, at read time: when a user opens the app, look up everyone they follow, fetch each followee's recent posts, and merge them newest-first.

Pull: writes are trivial (store one post). Every read fans in across all followees and merges — cost paid per read.
Pros
  • Writes are extremely cheap — one insert, no amplification
  • Always fresh; no stale precomputed copies
  • No wasted work for users who never open the app
Cons
  • Reads are expensive — merge across hundreds of followees every open
  • Read latency scales with how many you follow
  • Terrible for the read-heavy workload (20× more reads than writes)

Cost shape: write = O(1); read = O(followees × posts fetched) + merge. With a 20:1 read:write ratio, paying the big cost on the frequent operation is exactly backwards for the common case.

4.2 Push model (fan-out on write)

Do the work up front. When a user posts, immediately copy the post's id into the home timeline of every follower (a fan-out job, usually async via a queue). A read is then trivial: just return the reader's already-assembled timeline.

Push: one post triggers N timeline inserts (write amplification = N followers); a read is a single cheap lookup of the ready-made list.
# cost of one post under push
writes per post = N (N = author's follower count)
read per open = O(1) # return the precomputed list, newest-first

# average fleet-wide (from §2): amplification = avg followers = 200×
# a celebrity with N = 100,000,000 followers → 100M writes for ONE post
Pros
  • Reads are O(1) and extremely fast — perfect for read-heavy traffic
  • Feed is ready before the user asks — great tail latency
Cons
  • Write amplification = follower count → huge for popular accounts
  • Wastes work fanning out to inactive users who never open the app
  • A celebrity post = millions of writes (a "fan-out storm")
The celebrity problem

Push is wonderful until an account with 100,000,000 followers posts: that single action becomes 100 million timeline writes. Even at a dedicated 1,000,000 writes/sec that is ~100 seconds for one post — and celebrities post often, to overlapping audiences. Pure push cannot survive the tail of the follower distribution. That is the reason for the hybrid.

4.3 Hybrid model — the standard answer

Combine both by follower count: push for normal accounts (cheap fan-out, fast reads), but do not fan out posts from high-follower "celebrity" accounts — instead, pull those at read time and merge them into the reader's mostly-precomputed feed.

Hybrid: normal authors are pushed at write time; celebrities are not fanned out but pulled at read time and merged into the reader's mostly-precomputed feed.

The threshold — where to draw the line

Route by the author's follower_count against a threshold T:

# on publish
if author.follower_count < T: push (fan out to all followers now)
else: skip fan-out; store post only (pulled at read time)

# typical threshold: T on the order of 10,000–100,000 followers

Why a threshold and not "always hybrid"? The cost of pulling at read time is O(number of celebrities you follow) — and each reader follows only a handful of huge accounts (call it K, single digits to low tens). So the read merges one precomputed list plus K small celebrity queries — cheap. Meanwhile you've removed the giant fan-outs (the accounts above T) that would otherwise dominate the write load. You keep push's fast reads for 99%+ of authors and pull's cheap writes for the few whose fan-out would explode.

Why the threshold works — the math

The write cost you eliminate by not pushing a celebrity post is their entire follower count (up to 100M writes for one post). The read cost you add is only K extra small queries per feed open, where K = celebrities the reader follows (typically < 10). Trading up-to-100M writes-per-post for <10 extra reads-per-open is an overwhelmingly good deal — that asymmetry is the entire justification for the hybrid.

"I'd default to fan-out-on-write so the read-heavy path is O(1), but I'd cap it: any author above ~50k followers is not fanned out. Their posts are pulled at read time and merged into the reader's precomputed feed. That keeps writes bounded for celebrities while adding only a few small queries per feed open, since a reader follows very few huge accounts."

5. Timeline Storage & Cache

The push model needs somewhere fast to hold each user's precomputed timeline, plus a durable source of truth for the posts themselves.

Read path: fetch the capped list of post ids from the in-memory timeline cache, then hydrate bodies from the post cache (falling back to the sharded DB). Timelines store references; the post store holds content.
  • Timeline cache — a fast in-memory store (e.g. Redis) mapping user_id → an ordered list of recent post_ids (newest first), capped at ~800 entries per user. This is the ~3.2 TB from §2. It holds references, not bodies, so it's small and cheap to update on fan-out.
  • Post store (source of truth) — a durable, sharded database (shard by post_id or author_id) holding the actual post content. Timelines are a rebuildable cache; posts are not.
  • Post cache — hot post bodies cached so hydrating a feed page (turning ~20 ids into ~20 full posts) doesn't hit the DB every time. One post is read by many followers, so this cache hit-rate is excellent.

Pagination: cursor, not offset

The feed is a live list that grows at the top while the user scrolls. This makes classic OFFSET / page-number pagination wrong, not just slow.

Offset — broken here

  • LIMIT 20 OFFSET 40 = "skip 40, take 20."
  • If 5 new posts arrive at the top between page loads, everything shifts down 5 — page 3 now repeats items the user already saw on page 2 (and may skip others).
  • Also slow: the DB must scan and discard all OFFSET rows — cost grows with how deep you scroll.

Cursor — correct

  • The cursor is the id of the last item you saw: "give me 20 items older than post_id = X."
  • Stable under inserts — new posts at the top don't shift the window, so no duplicates or gaps.
  • Fast: it's an index seek to X then a sequential read, independent of scroll depth.
# cursor pagination — items strictly older than the cursor id
SELECT ... FROM home_timeline
 WHERE user_id = :me
   AND post_id < :cursor        # time-sortable id → "older than"
 ORDER BY post_id DESC
 LIMIT 20;                     # next_cursor = post_id of the last row
One-liner for the interview

"I'd paginate by cursor, not offset — the cursor is the last post id, so newly-arriving posts at the top can't cause duplicates or gaps, and it's an index seek instead of an O(offset) scan." Saying why (a growing list breaks offset) is the senior signal.

6. Ranking — Chronological vs. Ranked

Once you have the candidate posts, in what order do you show them? Two broad approaches; state the trade-off and keep the ML generic.

Two ordering approaches

Reverse-chronological

  • Newest first — sort by (time-sortable) post_id descending.
  • Simple, predictable, cheap; trivially matches the precomputed-list ordering.
  • Downside: a high-volume account can flood the feed; you see recency, not relevance.

Ranked (scored)

  • Compute a relevance score per candidate and sort by it, not just time.
  • Score blends generic signals: recency, affinity to the author, engagement (likes/replies), content type, predicted interaction.
  • Downside: more compute and infrastructure; ordering is less predictable to users.

A common structure is a two-stage funnel: candidate generation (gather the eligible recent posts — the pull/push output above) then scoring/ranking (a model orders the candidates). Storing a rank score alongside each timeline entry (the 4 bytes in §2) lets you keep the list roughly ordered without re-scoring on every read.

Keep it generic in the interview

You don't need any company's proprietary ranking details. Say "candidate generation then a ranking model over generic signals like recency, affinity, and engagement," note that ranking trades compute and predictability for relevance, and move on. Over-claiming specifics of a real product's model is a red flag, not a plus.

7. Notifications

Feeds are pull-when-you-open; notifications are the push-to-you side (someone liked your post, a followee went live, you were mentioned). They're a separate fan-out problem that reuses the same ideas.

  • An event (like, reply, mention, new follower) is emitted to a notification service via a queue — decoupled from the request that caused it, so the user's action returns immediately.
  • The service fans the event out to the target user(s) and hands off to multi-channel delivery: in-app notification list, push notification (mobile), maybe email/SMS — each channel a separate downstream with its own retry and rate limits.
  • The same celebrity problem reappears: "an account with millions of followers went live" shouldn't emit millions of individual notifications synchronously — batch, throttle, or collapse them.
Cross-links

Rate-limiting the per-channel senders is the previous chapter (Design a Rate Limiter) — token bucket per channel/provider. The async, multi-step handoff between channels is a workflow/queue problem; mention it and don't rebuild it inside the feed design.

8. Scaling & Bottlenecks

Hot celebrity fan-out

Already the headline: pushing a 100M-follower post is unworkable, so the hybrid pulls celebrities. Beyond that, keep celebrity posts in a small, very-hot cache (every reader who follows them pulls the same handful of recent posts), and cap how far back the pulled window reaches.

Thundering herd & cache stampede

Two herd shapes to name

Read stampede: a hugely popular post's cache entry expires and thousands of concurrent reads all miss and hit the DB at once. Fix with request coalescing (single-flight), staggered/jittered TTLs, and keeping hot posts pinned. Write herd: a celebrity going live could trigger a flood of downstream work; smooth it with queues and rate limits rather than doing it synchronously.

Cache invalidation & consistency

  • Timelines are a rebuildable cache: if a shard is lost, you can recompute a user's recent timeline from the follow graph + recent posts. Treat it as best-effort, not source of truth.
  • Deletes/edits: because ids are already fanned out, deleting a post can't easily un-write every copy. Common approach: filter deleted/edited posts at read/hydrate time (the post body is the source of truth), rather than chasing down every timeline copy.
  • Unfollow: similarly, you don't scrub the already-pushed entries — you filter at read time, or let them age out of the capped window.

Sharding the graph and the timelines

  • Timeline cache: shard by user_id (the reader). All of one user's timeline lives on one shard → a feed read is a single-shard lookup.
  • Post store: shard by post_id or author_id; time-sortable ids keep a single author's posts range-scannable.
  • Follow graph: store edges both directions and shard by the lookup key you need — by follower_id for "who I follow" (build feed) and by followee_id for "who follows me" (fan out). A very high-degree node (celebrity's follower set) is huge and hot — another reason celebrities skip fan-out.

9. Trade-offs — Pull vs. Push vs. Hybrid

DimensionPull (fan-out on read)Push (fan-out on write)Hybrid
Write costO(1) — one insertO(followers) — amplifiedO(followers) for normals; O(1) for celebrities
Read costO(followees) merge — expensiveO(1) lookup — cheapO(1) + merge K celebrities — cheap
FreshnessAlways freshSlight fan-out lag (secs)Celebs fresh; normals slight lag
Celebrity handlingFine (nothing to fan out)Breaks (fan-out storm)Solved (celebs pulled)
Wasted workNoneFans out to inactive usersReduced
ComplexityLowMediumHighest
Which when

Read-heavy at scale (the interview default): hybrid — push normals, pull celebrities. Small system / even follower counts / simplicity prized: pure push is fine. Very write-heavy, or feeds rarely read, or you can't tolerate stale copies: pull. The correct answer to "how would you build a feed" is almost always "push by default, hybrid to handle celebrities."

10. Interview Framing

How to drive the conversation

  1. Clarify (1–2 min): home timeline vs. profile timeline? scale (DAU, follower skew)? chronological or ranked? how fresh must it be (confirm eventual consistency is OK)?
  2. Estimate (§2): derive the 20:1 read:write ratio and the fan-out amplification — this motivates precomputing and exposes the celebrity problem before you're asked.
  3. Entities & APIs (§3): follow graph both ways, time-sortable post ids, three endpoints with cursor pagination.
  4. Feed generation (§4): present pull, then push, then land on hybrid with the follower-count threshold — this is the crux and where most of your time should go.
  5. Storage/cache, ranking, scaling (§5–§8): timeline cache of id-refs, cursor pagination, chronological vs. ranked, then celebrity/herd/invalidation/sharding.

What interviewers probe (with one-liners)

Q: Push or pull — which and why?
Default to push (fan-out on write) because the workload is ~20:1 read-heavy and eventual consistency is acceptable, so I pay at the rare write to make the frequent read O(1). But pure push breaks on high-follower accounts, so the real answer is hybrid.

Q: How do you handle a 100-million-follower account?
Don't fan it out. Above a follower threshold (~10k–100k) the author is a "celebrity": store the post but skip the push. Readers pull those few celebrities' recent posts at read time and merge them into their precomputed feed. That trades up-to-100M writes-per-post for a handful of extra small reads per feed open.

Q: What's the write amplification of push?
Exactly the author's follower count. Fleet-wide with ~200 avg followers that's ~200×: 100M posts/day become ~20B timeline writes/day (~231k/s average, ~460k/s peak). That amplification, concentrated in the follower-count tail, is the whole reason for the hybrid.

Q: Cursor vs. offset pagination — why does it matter?
The feed grows at the top while you scroll, so offset/page-number pagination shifts the window and repeats or skips items. A cursor = the last post id you saw ("older than X") is stable under inserts (no dupes/gaps) and is an index seek instead of an O(offset) scan.

Q: What consistency does a feed need?
Eventual. A post arriving in followers' feeds a second or two late is invisible to users, and that slack is exactly what lets us fan out asynchronously via a queue and treat timelines as a rebuildable cache.

Q: How do you store the timeline so reads are fast?
An in-memory timeline cache mapping user_id → a capped, newest-first list of post ids (references, not bodies — ~20 bytes each, ~800 per user ≈ 3.2 TB total). Reads fetch the id list, then hydrate bodies from a hot post cache backed by the sharded post DB.

Q: A post gets deleted or a user unfollows — how do you fix all the copies?
You don't chase every fanned-out copy. The post body is the source of truth, so you filter deleted/edited/unfollowed content at read/hydrate time and let stale ids age out of the capped window.

One-line summary

A social feed is a read-heavy fan-out problem: push posts into followers' precomputed timelines at write time so reads are O(1), but switch high-follower "celebrity" accounts to pull-and-merge at read time to bound write amplification. Store id-references in a capped in-memory timeline cache, hydrate from a sharded post store, paginate by cursor (not offset), and accept eventual consistency to keep the whole thing asynchronous.
