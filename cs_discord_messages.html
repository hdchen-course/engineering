Case Study: How Discord Stores Billions (then Trillions) of Messages

A single real system, told twice: the ~2016 move from MongoDB to Cassandra (documented in a January 2017 post), and the 2022 cut-over to ScyllaDB after Cassandra broke down at scale. Rewritten in our own words from Discord's two public engineering posts — every number below is quoted from those posts and cited in §8. When a fact wasn't stated, we leave it out rather than guess.
Why this case study is worth memorizing

It is the canonical "design a chat / message store" interview answer with real numbers attached. It shows the full arc a good candidate should narrate: a first datastore that fit the deadline, the specific way it broke, a principled second choice, the concrete pains that choice created, and finally a migration whose payoff you can quote in exact node counts and latencies. Read it as a template for how to reason about a datastore under growth.

1. The Problem — Message Storage at Scale

A chat product's central table is its messages. Every message must be stored durably, and — crucially — read back in two shapes: "give me the most recent messages in this channel," and "let me scroll backward through history." Discord's first version was, in their words, built in just under two months in early 2015, on a single MongoDB replica set, with everything indexed by a single compound index on channel_id and created_at.

The read/write shape that makes this hard

  • Reads are extremely random, and the read/write ratio is roughly 50/50. Unlike a feed you write once and read millions of times, a chat channel is read and written at similar rates, and users jump around to arbitrary points in history — so caching helps far less than you'd hope.
  • Per-channel volume varies enormously. Discord noted that voice-heavy servers are unlikely to send even 1,000 messages a year, private chats land in the 100 thousand to 1 million messages a year range, and large public servers easily accumulate millions of messages a year. Any data model has to hold both the tiny and the enormous channel gracefully.

Where MongoDB reached its limit

Overall message volume kept climbing for years: 40 million messages/day by July 2016, 100 million messages/day by December 2016, and well past 120 million a day by early 2017. But the MongoDB store hit its wall much earlier — and the breaking point was memory-bound, not disk-bound:

The failure, precisely

Around 100 million total stored messages (about November 2015), the data and its index could no longer fit in RAM, and latencies started to become unpredictable. When the working set stops fitting in memory, a random-read workload slows dramatically — every miss becomes a disk seek. That, not a single dramatic outage, is what forced the search for a new store.

So the requirements for the replacement were obvious: linear, predictable scaling as data grows past RAM; no single point of failure; self-healing operations a tiny team could run; and first-class support for the "read a range of recent messages in a channel" access pattern.

2. Why Cassandra (~2016) — the Data Model

Discord chose Cassandra: an open-source, wide-column store with no single point of failure (every node is equal), linear scalability by adding nodes, and automatic replication and repair — exactly the operational profile a 4-engineer team needs. They describe Cassandra as a "KKV" store: a two-part key made of a partition key that decides which node(s) hold the data, plus a clustering key that sorts rows within that partition.

First cut: (channel_id, message_id)

The natural primary key made channel_id the partition (so one channel's messages live together) and message_id the clustering key. The trick is that message_id is a Snowflake — a 64-bit, time-ordered ID — so ordering rows by message_id is the same as ordering them by time. "Give me the latest N messages" becomes a cheap, contiguous range scan from the end of the partition. No secondary time index needed.

The unbounded-partition problem

Partitioning purely by channel_id means a busy channel's partition grows forever. Cassandra advertises support for 2GB partitions, but Discord saw performance warnings once a partition passed 100MB. A single ever-growing partition also concentrates all of one channel's read and write load onto the same replica set — a hot spot waiting to happen.

The fix: bucketing by time

Discord added a bucket to the partition key — a static time window. They sized the bucket at roughly 10 days of messages, chosen so a typical partition stays comfortably under 100MB. The final compound primary key became:

PRIMARY KEY ( (channel_id, bucket) , message_id )
# partition key = (channel_id, bucket) → which node(s) hold the rows
# clustering key = message_id (Snowflake) → time-sorted order within the partition

To read a channel you compute the buckets spanning the time range you want and query those partitions. Because Snowflake IDs embed a timestamp, the client can derive bucket numbers directly from an ID or a time — no lookup table.

The wide-column layout: (channel_id, bucket) places rows on a node; message_id keeps them time-sorted, so "latest N" is a tail range scan.

What worked

The migration itself was fast and the win was immediate. Discord ran a small 12-node cluster with a replication factor of 3, carrying nearly 1 TB of compressed data on each node, operated by only 4 backend engineers with no dedicated DevOps. MongoDB was phased out within a week of the Cassandra rollout. Steady-state performance was strong: sub-millisecond writes and reads under 5 milliseconds, with alerts configured to fire only if the 95th-percentile response time went above 80 ms. The schema was lean — the full table had 16 columns, but an average message populated only 4 of them.

3. The Pain Points — Where Cassandra Hurt

Cassandra ran well for years, but as the cluster grew toward 177 nodes and trillions of messages by early 2022, a host of problems — most rooted in Cassandra being a JVM database with a garbage collector — turned operations into constant firefighting. The two public posts name them concretely.

Tombstones and deletes

Cassandra never deletes in place. A delete writes a tombstone — a marker that says "this is gone" — and tombstones live for a configurable window (10 days by default) until compaction removes them. Two problems followed:

  • Accidental tombstones. Writing a null into a column creates a tombstone. Discord's writes were generating 12 tombstones at a time, most of the time for no reason — because unset fields were being written as null. Reads then had to scan through those tombstones.
  • Reading through a graveyard. A now-famous incident: the Puzzles & Dragons Subreddit server deleted millions of messages, leaving a channel with essentially a single visible message. Loading that one message took 20 seconds, because Cassandra had to scan and skip millions of tombstones to find it. In the trillions post they add that the final token ranges held gigantic accumulations of tombstones that were never compacted away.

Discord's mitigations: they lowered the tombstone lifespan from 10 days to 2 days, ran repairs nightly, and taught the app to track and avoid reading empty buckets.

Hot partitions

The cascading hot partition

When a single channel/bucket partition took heavy read traffic — a popular channel, or many users hammering the same message — the replicas holding it became a hot spot, and because reads and writes used a quorum consistency level, the latency of that hot partition spread to every query hitting those serving nodes. One popular channel could degrade a whole slice of the cluster.

Compaction and GC — the operational tax

  • Compactions fell behind. Cassandra merges its on-disk files (SSTables) in the background; when that work couldn't keep up, engineers had to perform a manual "gossip dance" — taking nodes out of rotation so they could catch up on compaction without serving traffic.
  • Stop-the-world GC. Because Cassandra runs on the JVM, garbage collection pauses the node. Discord describes a pathological channel triggering a 10-second stop-the-world GC constantly, and, at fleet scale, super-long consecutive GC pauses that sometimes forced them to manually reboot nodes to recover.
The common root cause

Tombstone scans, unpredictable compaction, and GC pauses are all symptoms of the same thing: a JVM datastore whose latency is at the mercy of a garbage collector and background housekeeping. Tuning bought time, but the team was, in their words, spending too much of it babysitting the database. That framing is the bridge to the next decision.

4. The Migration to ScyllaDB

The replacement was ScyllaDB: a Cassandra-compatible database written in C++. Compatibility mattered enormously — the data model above could carry over — but the implementation is what solved the pain.

Why ScyllaDB

  • No garbage collector. Being written in C++ instead of Java, ScyllaDB has no garbage collector — which directly removes the stop-the-world pauses that were rebooting Cassandra nodes.
  • Shard-per-core architecture. ScyllaDB pins a shard to each CPU core, giving stronger workload isolation so one hot shard is less able to drag down the rest — a structural answer to the hot-partition cascade.
  • Faster repairs, easing the operational burden that a small team felt most.
  • Discord had already migrated all of their other databases to ScyllaDB by 2020; messages was the last holdout. The final blocker was performance of reverse-order reads, so Discord had ScyllaDB implement performant reverse queries, clearing the path.

The data services layer (Rust)

Rather than let the API talk to the database directly, Discord inserted intermediary data services written in Rust (on the Tokio async runtime), sitting between the API monolith and the database clusters. They contain no business logic — roughly one gRPC endpoint per database query — and exist to control how the datastore is accessed.

The key trick: request coalescing

The data services do request coalescing: if multiple users request the same row at the same time, the database is only queried once. Requests are steered with consistent hash-based routing keyed on the channel ID, so all requests for the same channel land on the same data-service instance and can be merged. This is precisely what defuses the hot-partition problem — a stampede on one popular channel collapses into a single backend read.

Request path after the migration: coalescing at the Rust data-service layer shields ScyllaDB from hot-partition stampedes.

The migration approach and speed

Discord provisioned a fresh ScyllaDB cluster on a "super-disk" storage topology (fast Local SSDs fronting persistent disk via RAID mirroring). Then came the data move. The out-of-the-box ScyllaDB Spark migrator estimated three months. The team instead rewrote the migrator in Rust in an afternoon, reusing their data-service library: it reads token ranges, checkpoints them locally in SQLite, and firehoses them into ScyllaDB. The new estimate dropped to nine days, running at speeds of up to 3.2 million messages per second. They validated by dual-reading a small percentage of traffic and comparing results before cutting over.

ScyllaDB became the primary messages database in May 2022.

Discord also reported the system smoothly handled the 2022 World Cup Final, whose message-send graph showed nine distinct traffic spikes, "handling it perfectly."

5. Architecture & Data Model — Before and After

Zooming out, the two eras share a data model but differ in what sits in front of the store and what the store is made of. The partition/clustering design is the durable idea; the front door and the engine are what changed.

Two eras, one data model: bucketed partition key + Snowflake clustering key survived; the store engine (JVM→C++) and the access layer (direct→Rust coalescing tier) changed.

6. Trade-offs & Lessons That Generalize

Strip away the brand names and this case study is a compact syllabus of message-store design. The transferable lessons:

  • Design partitions so they can't grow without bound. A pure channel_id partition grows forever and concentrates load; adding a time bucket caps partition size (Discord's target: under 100MB) and spreads load across time. Any "per-entity time-series" (chat, feeds, IoT, logs) wants a composite (entity, time_bucket) partition key.
  • Make your sort key double as your access pattern. Using a time-ordered ID (Snowflake) as the clustering key means the dominant query — "latest N" — is a cheap contiguous scan, with no separate index to maintain.
  • Model with the storage engine's delete semantics in mind. In an LSM/tombstone store, deletes and even null writes are not free — they leave markers reads must scan. Avoid writing nulls, keep tombstone lifetimes short, and steer reads away from empty ranges.
  • Beware the cost of GC at the datastore layer. A garbage-collected datastore couples tail latency to GC pauses; at scale those pauses become outages you reboot your way out of. A non-GC engine (C++) and a shard-per-core design removed a whole class of incidents.
  • Put a smart access tier in front of a dumb store. The Rust data services hold no business logic but add request coalescing and consistent-hash routing — turning a read stampede on a hot key into a single query. Solving hot partitions above the database is often cheaper than solving them inside it.
  • Buy-and-tune has a ceiling; sometimes you re-platform. Discord tuned Cassandra hard for years (tombstone TTLs, nightly repairs, the gossip dance) before concluding the pains were structural. Knowing when tuning is exhausted — and choosing a compatible replacement so the data model carries over — is the senior judgment call.
  • Right-size your tooling. An afternoon spent rewriting the migrator in a language they controlled turned a three-month migration into a nine-day one. Owning the critical-path tool paid for itself many times over.

7. Interview Angle — How to Use This

This case study maps directly onto the classic prompt "design a chat / message store" (and its cousins: design Slack, design comments, design a DM system). Citing a real system's numbers and evolution sends a strong signal.

What signal it sends

  • You reason about access patterns first (read/write ratio, "latest N" vs. history scroll) and let them drive the data model.
  • You know partition-key design cold — including the unbounded-partition trap and bucketing as the fix.
  • You can talk about a datastore's failure modes (tombstones, hot partitions, GC) rather than treating it as a black box.
  • You understand that migrations and operational cost are first-class design concerns, not afterthoughts.

Probe questions and crisp answers

Q: How would you store chat messages so "load the latest 50 in a channel" is cheap?
Partition by channel, cluster (sort) by a time-ordered message ID such as a Snowflake, so recent messages are a contiguous tail range within the partition. Read the tail; page backward for history. No secondary time index needed.

Q: A busy channel's partition grows forever — what breaks and how do you fix it?
An unbounded partition blows past the store's comfortable size (Discord saw warnings past ~100MB) and concentrates all of that channel's read/write load on one replica set. Fix it by adding a time bucket to the partition key — e.g. ~10 days of messages per bucket — so partitions stay bounded and load spreads across time. Derive the bucket from the message's timestamp so no lookup is needed.

Q: One popular channel is spiking latency for unrelated queries. Why, and what do you do?
That's a hot partition: heavy reads on one partition overload its serving nodes, and with quorum reads the latency bleeds into every query on those nodes. Put an access tier in front that does request coalescing (collapse concurrent identical reads into one backend query) with consistent-hash routing so same-key requests merge — and/or pick a store with shard-per-core isolation so a hot shard can't starve the rest.

Q: Why can deletes make a database slower?
In LSM/tombstone stores a delete writes a tombstone that reads must scan over until compaction reclaims it; even writing nulls creates them. A channel with millions of deleted messages made Discord's single-surviving-message read take ~20 seconds. Mitigate by not writing nulls, shortening tombstone TTLs, running repairs, and skipping known-empty ranges — or choose a delete-friendlier model.

Q: When do you re-platform a database instead of tuning it?
When the pain is structural, not configurational. Discord tuned Cassandra for years, but GC pauses, compaction backlogs, and tombstone scans were inherent to a JVM LSM store at their scale. They moved to a Cassandra-compatible C++ engine (ScyllaDB) so the data model carried over, cutting 177 nodes to 72 and tightening p99s. The sign: you're rebooting nodes and babysitting background work rather than shipping features.
